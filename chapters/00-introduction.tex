\section{Objective and Scope}


The primary objective of this dissertation is to provide a solid review of \textit{volatility}, aiming to fill the gap between readers who come from mathematics --- and who often lack expertise in finance --- and readers who come from finance, and who may not always see the depth of the machinery they rely on. For this reconciliatory purpose, the text keeps a clear and rigorous mathematical mindset while maintaining continuous references to practical and financial interpretations.




\hyperref[part:part1]{Part I} aims to provide a thorough introduction to the subject, and is divided along exactly the line this dissertation sets out to bridge. Chapter \ref{ch:foundations} develops the probabilistic machinery: measure-theoretic preliminaries, Brownian motion, the Itô integral and stochastic differential equations. Particular attention is paid there to the regularity of Brownian trajectories, since it is precisely the delicate balance between roughness and tractability --- non-vanishing quadratic variation on the one hand, unbounded total variation on the other --- that both makes Itô's theory necessary and marks the limits of its reach. Chapter \ref{ch:finance} then builds the financial theory on top of it: contingent claims and arbitrage, the fundamental theorems of asset pricing, the Black--Scholes framework, and the local and stochastic volatility models that were devised to repair it. A reader already fluent in stochastic calculus may begin at Chapter \ref{ch:finance}; a reader who wants only the mathematics may stop before it.

\hyperref[part:part2]{Part II} is devoted to more recent developments in volatility modelling, including Rough Paths and Rough Volatility theories (Chapter \ref{ch:roughpaths}). The latter is introduced from a general and mathematical perspective: although it finds direct applications in finance, the theory of rough paths has far-reaching implications in other areas of mathematics as well. These chapters aim to highlight the necessity of more sophisticated tools to describe and operate in the market.

Finally, these theoretical constructs are brought to practice in Chapter \ref{ch:applications}, where two calibrations are performed against the same market smiles: an implied volatility surface fitted by a physics-informed neural network, on which the static no-arbitrage conditions are imposed as penalties and, on the extrapolated wings, proved outright; and the rough Heston model of Chapter \ref{ch:roughpaths}. Here, the models are numerically implemented and compared, providing an applied perspective and illustrating the strengths and challenges associated with each approach. Some final conclusions are left for Chapter \ref{ch:conclusions}, where a general overview of the work done will be given, along with reflections on possible directions for further research.

\section{A note on context}

\begin{center}
    \centering
    \textit{The miracle of the appropriateness of the language of mathematics for the formulation of the laws of physics is a wonderful gift which we neither understand nor deserve.}
\end{center}

Eugene Wigner expressed his admiration in \textit{The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences}, his 1960 article in \textit{Communications on Pure and Applied Mathematics} \cite{wigner1960}. It is a sentiment many would agree with: the effectiveness of mathematics in describing Nature is both remarkable and fascinating. Moreover, over the last century its branches have reached into areas where, not so long ago, mathematicians were considered too abstract to be of much use in business terms.

In 1827, Robert Brown, observing under a microscope the minute particles released by pollen grains suspended in water, noticed something unexpected: the particles wandered along irregular, hard-to-describe paths. Physics was not yet equipped to tackle such a problem. What was needed belonged to what we now call \textit{statistical mechanics}, the study of collective behaviour of particles and molecules, intimately tied to \textit{thermodynamics.} The decisive step came later, in 1905, when Albert Einstein provided a theory of this motion --- the same year in which he also explained the photoelectric effect, introduced special relativity and gave the first statement of the mass--energy equivalence formula, $E=mc^2$. What a year.

Far from pollen grains, a parallel story was unfolding in finance. In 1900, Louis Bachelier, a doctoral student of Henri Poincaré, defended a thesis on the modelling of stock prices. His model is what we would now call an arithmetic Brownian motion, and it rested on shaky probabilistic foundations: Kolmogorov and his peers were not yet born, and even Lebesgue had not yet published his work on measure theory. Worse, it allowed negative prices --- a point critics did not miss.

The common thread in both stories is \emph{Brownian motion}. This stochastic process is a central object in probability: geometers find its fractal dimension fascinating, while probabilists value the rich family of processes built upon it. The idea of treating “random noise” as a genuine mathematical object took shape gradually, but it was Kiyoshi Itô’s work on \emph{stochastic integration} that truly changed the picture.

Finance came to the subject later, but with undeniable impact. Today mathematics is everywhere in banks, consultancy and audit firms, underpinning the valuation of derivatives, that is, assigning a \emph{fair price} (according to market data) to contracts involving random future cash flows. Pricing is the orientation of this dissertation. It is only one of several uses of the same machinery --- risk management and portfolio optimisation are others --- but it is the one in which the mathematics is most visible.
