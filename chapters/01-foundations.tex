\chapter{Stochastic Foundations} \label{ch:foundations}

We assume the reader is familiar with the foundations of probability theory. 
Standard references include, for example, Billingsley's classic text 
\cite{billingsley1995}, as well as the treatments in \cite{durrett2019} and \cite{kallenberg2021}. 
Nevertheless, we briefly recall the basic probabilistic framework in order to fix 
notation and ensure clarity.

\medskip

We will work on a probability space $\PSpace$, with $\Omega$ the sample space, $\mathcal{F}$ a
$\sigma$-algebra of events, and $\PP$ a probability measure. A \textbf{random variable} is a
measurable map $X : (\Omega,\mathcal{F}) \to (E,\mathcal{E})$, the target usually being 
$(\R^n,\mathcal{B}(\R^n))$, and particularly $(\R,\mathcal{B}(\R))$ in most of our applications.

\begin{convention*}
Every probability space in this dissertation is assumed \emph{complete}: if $A \in \mathcal{F}$ has
$\PP(A)=0$, then every $B \subseteq A$ lies in $\mathcal{F}$ and has $\PP(B) = 0$. Completeness is not a formality, and we shall see in Definition \ref{def:usualconditions} exactly
what it buys.
\end{convention*}

%  For the moment it is enough to record that this is the first of several points at which
% the measure-theoretic language earns its place: it is not a technical convenience but the only
% setting in which path regularity, filtrations and martingale properties can be discussed at all.

\begin{convention*}[The symbols $\square$ and $\blacksquare$]\label{conv:blacksquare}
A result whose \emph{statement} ends with a hollow $\square$ is quoted \textbf{without proof}, a
reference being given in every case. A filled $\blacksquare$ closes an \emph{argument}: it marks the
end of a proof, whether that proof is given in full, reduced to a sketch, or deferred to Appendix
\ref{app:proofs}. The convention is followed throughout.
\end{convention*}


\section{Probabilistic Preliminaries}\label{sec:preliminaries}

The notion of \emph{stochastic process} generalises that of a single random variable by allowing evolution in an external parameter, usually interpreted as time. To accommodate both discrete-time and continuous-time settings in a unified framework, we let \(\Tset\) denote the \textbf{index set}, representing time. Typically, \(\Tset\) is a subset of \(\R\), such as:
\[
\Tset \in \{ \N, \N_0, \Z, [0, T], [0, \infty), \R \},
\]
depending on the application.

\begin{definition}
Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \PP)\) be a (complete) probability space, and let \(\Tset \) be a time index set. A \textbf{stochastic process} is a collection of random variables \(\{ X_t : t \in \Tset \}\) defined on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \PP)\) with values in a measurable space \((E, \mathcal{E})\), such that the map
\[
X : \Tset \times \Omega \to E, \quad (t, \omega) \mapsto X_t(\omega)
\]
is jointly measurable with respect to \(\mathcal{B}(\Tset) \otimes \mathcal{F}\). In particular:
\begin{itemize}
    \item For each fixed \(t \in \Tset\), the function \(\omega \mapsto X_t(\omega)\) is a random variable on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \PP)\).
    \item For each fixed \(\omega \in \Omega\), the function \(t \mapsto X_t(\omega)\) is called the \emph{sample path} or \emph{trajectory} of the process.
\end{itemize}
We may denote the process by \(X = \{X_t\}_{t \in \Tset}\), and abbreviate \(X_t(\omega)\) as \(X(t)\) or simply \(X_t\) when context allows. We say $X$ is a \textit{discrete-time} process whenever $\Tset$ is $\N$ or $\Z$, and a \textit{continuous-time} process if $\Tset$ is $[0,T]$ for some $T>0$ or $[0,\infty)$. This is a statement about the index set only, and is independent of whether the sample paths are continuous: the Poisson process of Example \ref{example:randomwalk} is a continuous-time process with discontinuous paths.
\end{definition}

\vspace{-0.15cm}

This formulation includes many important examples such as Markov chains, Poisson processes, and Brownian motion. Note that the nature of the index set \(\Tset\) often dictates the regularity assumptions or path properties we may require (e.g., right-continuity, càdlàg, continuity in probability, etc.).  Analysing the dynamic evolution of these processes requires us to quantify their expected future behaviour given currently available information. This idea is contained in the \textbf{conditional expectation}. Its existence relies on the following profound result:

\begin{theorem}[Radon--Nikodym]\label{thm:radon-nikodym}
    Let \(\nu\) and \(\lambda\) be two \(\sigma\)-finite measures on the same measurable space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\). Suppose that \(\nu\) is \emph{absolutely continuous} with respect to \(\lambda\), denoted \(\nu \ll \lambda\); that is,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:abscont}
\lambda(A) = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \nu(A) = 0, \quad \text{for all } A \in \mathcal{F}.
\end{equation}

\noindent Then there exists a measurable function \(f : \Omega \to [0, \infty)\), unique up to $\lambda$-null sets, such that for all \(A \in \mathcal{F}\),
\begin{equation} \label{eq:radonnikodym}
\nu(A) = \int_A f \, d\lambda.
\end{equation}
This function \(f\) is called the \emph{Radon--Nikodym derivative} of \(\nu\) with respect to \(\lambda\), and is denoted by $f = d\nu/d\lambda$. \hfill $\square$
\end{theorem}

% The letter $\mu$ is deliberately avoided here. From Section \ref{sec:stochasticcalculus} onwards it will denote a drift coefficient and nothing else; abstract measures are $\nu$ and $\lambda$ throughout.

We shall need the statement in slightly greater generality than above, since the measure we are about to construct might not be positive.

\begin{corollary}[Signed version]\label{cor:radon-nikodym-signed}
Let $\nu$ be a finite \emph{signed} measure on $(\Omega,\mathcal{F})$ and $\lambda$ a $\sigma$-finite positive measure with $\nu \ll \lambda$. Then there exists $f \in L^1(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\lambda)$, real-valued and unique up to $\lambda$-null sets, satisfying \eqref{eq:radonnikodym}.
\end{corollary}

\vspace{-0.1cm}

\textbf{\textit{Proof:}} Apply the Jordan decomposition $\nu = \nu^+ - \nu^-$ into mutually singular finite positive measures. Both $\nu^{\pm} \ll \lambda$, so Theorem \ref{thm:radon-nikodym} yields non-negative densities $f^{\pm}$, and $f := f^+ - f^-$ works; integrability follows from $\int (f^+ + f^-)\,d\lambda = |\nu|(\Omega) < \infty$. \hfill $\blacksquare$

As a consequence, if \(X \in L^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \PP)\) (that is, $\int |X| \, d\PP$ is finite) and \(\mathcal{G} \subseteq \mathcal{F}\) is a sub-\(\sigma\)-algebra, the \emph{conditional expectation} of \(X\) given \(\mathcal{G}\), denoted \(\E[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\), is the unique (up to \(\PP\)-a.e. equality) \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable function satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{eq:condexpdefining}
\int_G \E[X \mid \mathcal{G}] \, d\PP = \int_G X \, d\PP, \quad \text{for all } G \in \mathcal{G}.
\end{equation}

\vspace{-0.1cm}

That such a function exists at all is exactly an application of the corollary just proved. Define the set function \(\nu : \mathcal{G} \to \R\) by
\begin{equation}
\nu(G) := \int_G X \, d\PP, \quad \text{for } G \in \mathcal{G}.
\end{equation}
Since \(X\) is integrable, \(\nu\) is a finite \emph{signed} measure on \((\Omega, \mathcal{G})\), and it is absolutely continuous with respect to \(\PP|_{\mathcal{G}}\), the restriction of \(\PP\) to \(\mathcal{G}\). By Corollary \ref{cor:radon-nikodym-signed} it therefore admits a \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable density with respect to \(\PP|_{\mathcal{G}}\), and that density is precisely the object characterised by \eqref{eq:condexpdefining}.



Conditional expectation inherits the properties one would expect of an averaging operator: it
preserves expectations and order, it is linear, it acts as the identity on $\mathcal{G}$-measurable
variables and as the plain expectation on independent ones, known factors may be taken out of it, and
it satisfies the tower property $\E[X\mid\mathcal{H}] =
\E[\E[X\mid\mathcal{G}]\mid\mathcal{H}]$ for $\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{G}$. These,
together with the conditional forms of the Fatou, monotone and dominated convergence theorems, are
recorded as Proposition \ref{prop:condexpproperties} in Appendix \ref{app:auxiliary}; we shall use
them throughout without comment. Two further properties, however, are used more than all the others
combined and are stated here.

\begin{proposition}[Conditional Jensen and the $L^2$ projection]\label{prop:condjensenl2}
Let $\mathcal{G} \subseteq \mathcal{F}$ be a sub-$\sigma$-algebra. Then:
\begin{itemize}
    \item[i)] (\textbf{Conditional Jensen's inequality}) If $\phi : \R \to \R$ is convex and both $X$ and $\phi(X)$ are integrable, then
    \[
        \phi\bigl(\E[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\bigr) \leq \E[\phi(X) \mid \mathcal{G}]
        \qquad \text{almost surely.}
    \]

    \item[ii)] (\textbf{$L^2$ projection}) If $X \in L^2(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\PP)$, then $\E[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ is the orthogonal projection of $X$ onto the closed subspace $L^2(\Omega,\mathcal{G},\PP)$. Equivalently, it is the unique $\mathcal{G}$-measurable $Y$ minimising $\E[(X-Y)^2]$, and
    \[
        \E\bigl[(X - \E[X\mid\mathcal{G}])\,Z\bigr] = 0 \quad \text{for every } Z \in L^2(\Omega,\mathcal{G},\PP).
    \]
\end{itemize}
\hfill $\square$
\end{proposition}

% The numbering is retained from Proposition \ref{prop:condexpproperties} for ease of reference.

% Property \textit{i)} is what guarantees that $X^2$ is a submartingale whenever $X$ is a martingale, a fact invoke in Subsection \ref{subsec:martingales} and again in the Doob--Meyer decomposition. Property \textit{ii)} allows for an interpretation and characterization as a projection, that is, an object that minimizes a certain distance.

% Property \textit{ii)} deserves more than emphasis. It says that conditioning is nothing more exotic than orthogonal projection in a Hilbert space, and once this is seen, a good deal of what follows stops looking like a series of coincidences. The Itô isometry of Theorem \ref{thm:itoisometry} will be an isometry between two Hilbert spaces; the density argument that extends the stochastic integral from step processes to all of $L^2_{\mathrm{ad}}$ is the standard extension of a bounded operator from a dense subspace; and the Martingale Representation Theorem of Subsection \ref{subsec:measurechanges} will say that a certain family of stochastic integrals is not merely orthogonal but \emph{complete}. The geometry is the same throughout.

In the study of stochastic processes, it is essential to formalise how information accumulates over time. This is done through the concept of a \textbf{filtration}, which models the \emph{evolution of knowledge} or \emph{information available} up to each time \(t\).

\begin{definition}[Filtration]
Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \PP)\) be a probability space and let \(\Tset\) be a time index set. A \textbf{filtration} is a family \(\{\mathcal{F}_t\}_{t \in \Tset}\) of sub-\(\sigma\)-algebras of \(\mathcal{F}\) satisfying:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{F}_s \subseteq \mathcal{F}_t \subseteq \mathcal{F} \quad \text{for all } s \leq t.
\end{equation}
That is, the filtration is \emph{increasing} in \(t\). We interpret \(\mathcal{F}_t\) as the collection of events whose outcomes are known or observable up to  \(t\). A stochastic process \(X = \{X_t\}_{t \in \Tset}\) is said to be \textbf{adapted} to the filtration \(\{\mathcal{F}_t\}\) if, for each \(t \in \Tset\), the random variable \(X_t\) is measurable with respect to \(\mathcal{F}_t\).  We say that $\FPSpace$ is a \textbf{filtered probability space}.
\end{definition}

% Bare adaptedness turns out to be slightly too weak for comfort. Two further requirements are so universally imposed that they have acquired a name of their own, and we impose them here once and for all rather than invoking them piecemeal later.

\begin{definition}[The usual conditions]\label{def:usualconditions}
A filtered probability space $\FPSpace$ is said to satisfy the \textbf{usual conditions} if
\begin{itemize}
    \item[i)] $\mathcal{F}_0$ contains every $\PP$-null set of $\mathcal{F}$ (\emph{completeness}); and
    \item[ii)] the filtration is \emph{right-continuous}, that is,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:rightcontinuous}
    \mathcal{F}_t = \mathcal{F}_{t^+} := \bigcap_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{F}_{t + 1/n}, \qquad t \in [0,T).
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{convention}\label{conv:usualconditions}
From this point onwards, every filtered probability space is assumed to satisfy the usual conditions.
\end{convention}

Completeness is what allows us to modify a process on a null set without leaving the filtration, as we shall do whenever we choose a continuous version of an object defined only up to
indistin\-guishability. Right-continuity is what makes first hitting times of open sets into
stopping times; it does explicit work in Proposition \ref{prop:stoppingtimechar} below, and
again in the Doob--Meyer decomposition of Subsection \ref{subsec:martingales}. 

% Neither is automatic for a natural filtration $\mathcal{F}^X_t$, but both can always be arranged: one replaces $\mathcal{F}^X_t$ by the $\PP$-completion of $\mathcal{F}^X_{t^+}$, the so-called \emph{augmented} filtration, and for the filtrations generated by the processes we shall study this augmentation changes nothing of substance.

% \begin{remark}[Modification versus indistinguishability]\label{rem:modification}
% A related piece of hygiene, which we shall need whenever a theorem produces ``a continuous version'' of a process. Two processes $X$ and $Y$ on the same space are said to be \emph{modifications} of each other if $\PP(X_t = Y_t) = 1$ for every fixed $t$, and \emph{indistinguishable} if $\PP(X_t = Y_t \text{ for all } t) = 1$. The second is strictly stronger: the exceptional null set is allowed to depend on $t$ in the first case, and a union of uncountably many null sets need not be null. The two notions do coincide when both processes have right-continuous paths, which is the situation we shall almost always be in --- but the coincidence is a small theorem, not a definition, and we shall be careful to say which of the two we mean.
% \end{remark}

Recall that if \(\chi\) is a real-valued random variable on a probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \PP)\), then the \(\sigma\)-algebra generated by \(\chi\) is denoted \(\sigma(\chi)\) and defined as:
\begin{equation}
\sigma(\chi) := \{ A \in \mathcal{F} : A = \chi^{-1}(B) \text{ for some } B \in \mathcal{B}(\R) \}.
\end{equation}

\begin{example}[Natural Filtration]
Given a stochastic process \(X = \{X_t\}_{t \in \Tset}\), the \textbf{natural filtration} generated by \(X\) is defined by:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{F}_t^X := \sigma(X_s : s \leq t),
\end{equation}
the smallest \(\sigma\)-algebra with respect to which all variables \(X_s\) for \(s \leq t\) are measurable. This filtration encodes exactly the information revealed by observing the process up to time \(t\).
\end{example}

\begin{definition}[Stopping time]\label{stoppingTime}
Let $(\Omega,\mathcal{F}, \PP)$ be a probability space carrying a filtration $\{\mathcal{F}_t\}_{t\in\Tset}$. A mapping $\tau:\Omega \rightarrow \Tset$ is a \textit{stopping time} if
\[
\{\omega : \tau(\omega) \leq t\} \in \mathcal{F}_t \qquad \text{for each } t \in \Tset .
\]
\end{definition}

% Stating the definition on a general index set costs nothing and saves a parenthesis. Throughout this dissertation $\Tset = [0,T]$, so that every stopping time is automatically bounded by $T$; the single place where the infinite horizon $\Tset = [0,\infty)$ is needed is Theorem \ref{thm:optionalstopping}, which says so explicitly, and the reason it is needed there is exactly that boundedness is what would otherwise make its hypotheses vacuous.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:stoppingtimechar}
 Let \(\Filt\) be a right-continuous filtration. Then, a random variable \(\tau : \Omega \to [0, T]\) is a stopping time with respect to \(\Filt\) if and only if  
\[
\{ \, \omega \in \Omega : \tau(\omega) < t \, \} \in \mathcal{F}_t, 
\quad \text{for all } t \in [0, T].
\]
\end{proposition}

\textbf{\textit{Proof:}} Suppose first that $\tau$ is a stopping time. Since
$\{\tau < t\} = \bigcup_{n \geq 1}\{\tau \leq t - 1/n\}$ and each $\{\tau \leq t-1/n\}$ lies in
$\mathcal{F}_{t-1/n} \subseteq \mathcal{F}_t$, the union lies in $\mathcal{F}_t$. This implication
uses nothing beyond monotonicity of the filtration.

Conversely, suppose $\{\tau < t\} \in \mathcal{F}_t$ for every $t$. Then for each $n \geq 1$ we have
$\{\tau < t + 1/n\} \in \mathcal{F}_{t+1/n}$, and since $\{\tau \leq t\} = \bigcap_{n\geq1}\{\tau < t + 1/n\}$,
\[
\{\tau \leq t\} \;\in\; \bigcap_{n \geq 1}\mathcal{F}_{t+1/n} \;=\; \mathcal{F}_{t^+} \;=\; \mathcal{F}_t,
\]
the last equality being exactly the right-continuity \eqref{eq:rightcontinuous}. \hfill $\blacksquare$

\medskip

Note that the converse consumes right-continuity in full, and fails without it.

% This is the first of several places where the usual conditions of Definition
% \ref{def:usualconditions} do real work rather than merely tidy up.

\begin{example}\label{example_stoppingTimes}
    Let $X$ be a measurable adapted process on $[0,T]$ with $\int_0^T |X_s|^2\,ds < \infty$ almost surely. For $n \in \N$ set
    \[
    A_n(\omega) =  \Bigl\{ t \in [0,T] : \int_0^t |X_s(\omega)|^2\,ds > n\Bigr\},
    \]
    and let $\tau_n(\omega) = \inf A_n(\omega)$ if $A_n(\omega) \neq \emptyset$, and $\tau_n(\omega) = T$ otherwise. Since $t \mapsto \int_0^t |X_s|^2 ds$ is continuous and non-decreasing, the threshold $n$ is exceeded strictly before $t$ if and only if it is exceeded at some rational time strictly before $t$, so that for $t\in (0,T]$
    \begin{equation}
        \{\tau_n < t\} = \bigcup_{\substack{0<r<t \\ r \in \mathbb{Q}}} \Bigl\{\int_0^r |X_s|^2\,ds > n\Bigr\} \in \mathcal{F}_t,
    \end{equation}
    whereas $\{\tau_n < 0\} = \emptyset \in \mathcal{F}_0$. By Proposition \ref{prop:stoppingtimechar}, $\tau_n$ is a stopping time.

     The sequence $(\tau_n)_{n\in\N}$ just constructed is the \emph{localising sequence} that will allow us, in Subsection \ref{subsec:itointegral}, to define the stochastic integral for integrands that are square-integrable pathwise but not in expectation; it is the prototype of every localisation argument in this chapter.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[First hitting time]\label{example:hittingtime}
    Let $X$ be a right-continuous process adapted to $\{\mathcal{F}_t\}$. If $U \subseteq \R$ is an \emph{open} set, then the \textit{first hitting time}
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:hittingtime}
        \tau_U(\omega) = \inf\{t>0 : X_t(\omega) \in U\}
    \end{equation}
    is a stopping time. Indeed, since $U$ is open and $X$ is right-continuous, the path enters $U$ strictly before $t$ if and only if it lies in $U$ at some \emph{rational} time strictly before $t$: if $X_r \in U$ for some $r<t$, right-continuity places $X$ in the open set $U$ throughout a nonempty interval $[r,r+\epsilon)$, which contains a rational. Hence
    \begin{equation}
        \{\tau_U < t\} = \bigcup_{\substack{0<r<t \\ r \in \mathbb{Q}}} \{X_r \in U\} \;\in\; \mathcal{F}_t ,
    \end{equation}
    and Proposition \ref{prop:stoppingtimechar} applies.
\end{example}

% The hypotheses are doing real work, and it is worth seeing where. Openness of $U$ is what allows the union to be taken over a \emph{countable} set of times: for a \emph{closed} target the same argument fails, since a path may touch a closed set at a single irrational instant and at no rational one, and one needs continuity of the paths rather than mere right-continuity to recover the conclusion. For a general Borel $U$ neither hypothesis suffices, and that $\tau_U$ is a stopping time at all is the \emph{début theorem} --- a genuinely deep result resting on the measurable projection theorem, and one of the few places in this subject where the measure-theoretic machinery is not merely bookkeeping. We shall need only the open case, which is the elementary one.

    
    

\subsection{Fundamental Distributions}\label{subsec:FundamentalDistr}

Before presenting some cornerstone distributions, recall that the \textbf{characteristic function} of a (real) random variable $X$ is defined by
\begin{equation}
\charf_X(u) := \E[e^{i u X}], \quad u \in \R.
\end{equation}
It is the Fourier transform of the probability distribution of $X$.

Its elementary properties (normalisation, uniform continuity, Hermitian symmetry, behaviour under
affine maps and under sums of independent variables, and the correspondence between differentiability
at the origin and existence of moments) are collected in Proposition \ref{prop:charfnproperties}
of Appendix \ref{app:auxiliary}, and are used below without further comment.

\begin{theorem}[Fourier inversion]\label{thm:fourierinversion}
Let $X$ be a random variable with distribution function $F_X$. If $\charf_X$ is integrable, then $F_X$ admits a continuous density $f_X$ given by
\begin{equation}
f_X(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{\R} e^{-i u x} \charf_X(u) \, du.
\end{equation}
\qedopen
\end{theorem}

In the general case, without assuming a density, the distribution function $F_X$ can still be recovered via the \emph{Lévy inversion formula}: for any $\alpha < \beta$ that are \emph{continuity points} of $F_X$,
\begin{equation}\label{levyInversionFormula}
F_X(\beta) - F_X(\alpha) = \lim_{R \to \infty} \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-R}^R \frac{e^{-i \alpha u} - e^{-i \beta u}}{i u} \, \charf_X(u) \, du.
\end{equation}
The restriction to continuity points is necessary and not a technicality: at a jump of $F_X$ the right-hand side converges to the average of the left and right limits rather than to $F_X$ itself. Since $F_X$ is monotone it has at most countably many discontinuities, so continuity points are dense and the formula determines $F_X$ everywhere by right-continuity.

We deduce that if two random variables $X$ and $Y$ have the same characteristic function, i.e.\ $\charf_X(u) = \charf_Y(u)$ for all $u \in \R$, then $X$ and $Y$ have the same distribution.

\begin{theorem}[Lévy's Continuity Theorem]\label{theorem:LevyContinuity}
Let $\{X_n\}_{n \in \N}$ be a sequence of random variables with characteristic functions $\charf_{X_n}$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item If $X_n \xrightarrow{d} X$ for some random variable $X$, then $\charf_{X_n}(u) \to \charf_X(u)$ for every $u \in \R$.
  \item Conversely, suppose $\charf_{X_n}(u) \to \charf(u)$ for every $u \in \R$, where $\charf : \R\to\Cx$ is \emph{some} function continuous at $u=0$. Then $\charf$ is the characteristic function of a random variable $X$, and $X_n \xrightarrow{d} X$. \hfill $\square$
\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

 In the converse direction one is \emph{not} allowed to assume that the limit $\charf$ is a characteristic function; that is the conclusion, not a hypothesis. Continuity at the origin is what rules out mass escaping to infinity, and it is a genuine restriction on $\charf$. Note that if one already knew $\charf$ to be a characteristic function the requirement would be vacuous, since characteristic functions of real variables are uniformly continuous.

We now describe certain distributions of widespread importance and usefulness. The first place must be given to the normal distribution.

\medskip

\begin{example}[Normal Distribution]
Let $\mu \in \R$ and $\sigma > 0$, and define the real-valued random variable $X \colon \Omega \to \R$ with distribution $X \sim \Normal(\mu, \sigma^2),$
that is, $X$ has the probability density function
\begin{equation}
f_X(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \sigma^2}} \exp\left( -\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right), \quad x \in \R.
\end{equation}
Then $X$ is said to be normally distributed with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$. Thus, a normal distribution is characterised by its first two moments.
\end{example}

The normal distribution is fundamental in probability theory and statistics. It appears as the limiting distribution in the Central Limit Theorem and models many naturally occurring phenomena. Its characteristic function adopts the form
\begin{equation}
\charf_X(u) = \exp\left( i u \mu - \tfrac{1}{2} \sigma^2 u^2 \right).
\end{equation}
The resemblance of its density to the \textit{heat kernel} (see \cite{evans2010}) is not a coincidence, and will be explained in Subsection \ref{section:itoDiff}.

\begin{example}[Multivariate Normal Distribution]\label{example:multivariatenormal}
An $\R^d$-valued random vector $X$ follows a \textbf{multivariate normal distribution} with
mean $\mu \in \R^d$ and covariance $\Sigma \in \R^{d\times d}$, written
$X \sim \Normal_d(\mu,\Sigma)$, if $a\transp X \sim \Normal(a\transp\mu,\, a\transp\Sigma a)$ for
every $a \in \R^d$.
\end{example}

Here $\Sigma$ is necessarily symmetric positive semi-definite. If it is invertible the law is
absolutely continuous on $\R^d$ with density
$f_X(x) = (2\pi)^{-d/2}\det(\Sigma)^{-1/2}\exp\bigl(-\tfrac12 (x-\mu)\transp\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\bigr)$;
if it is singular, $X$ is supported on a lower-dimensional affine subspace. In either case the
characteristic function is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvncharfn}
\charf_X(u) = \E[e^{i u\transp X}] = \exp\left( i u\transp \mu - \tfrac{1}{2} u\transp \Sigma u \right), \quad u \in \R^d.
\end{equation}

The characteristic function $\charf_X$ of \eqref{eq:mvncharfn} extends to an entire analytic function on $\mathbb{C}^d$. Moreover, a sequence of random vectors $X_n \sim \Normal_d(\mu_n, \Sigma_n)$ converges in distribution to $\Normal_d(\mu, \Sigma)$ if and only if $\mu_n \to \mu$ in $\R^d$ and $\Sigma_n \to \Sigma$ entrywise.

Any symmetric positive semi-definite matrix $\Sigma$ admits a matrix factor $\Sigma = L L^{\top}$ (e.g., via Cholesky or spectral decomposition), yielding the representation $X \stackrel{d}{=} \mu + L Z$ for $Z \sim \mathcal{N}_d(0, I_d)$. What sets the multivariate case apart from 1D is spatial geometry: $L$ acts as an anisotropic scaling and rotation, deforming standard spherical contours into oriented ellipsoids therefore introducing directional cross-correlations.

The following are easily verified through Theorem \ref{theorem:LevyContinuity} (or by evaluating characteristic functions directly via \eqref{eq:mvncharfn}):

\begin{itemize}
    \item Let $X \sim \mathcal{N}_d(\mu_X, \Sigma_X)$ and $Y \sim \mathcal{N}_d(\mu_Y, \Sigma_Y)$ be two \emph{independent} multivariate normal random vectors in $\mathbb{R}^d$.  For matrices $A, B \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d}$ and a vector $c \in \mathbb{R}^m$, 
    \[
    AX + BY + c \sim \mathcal{N}_m\bigl(A\mu_X + B\mu_Y + c,\, A\Sigma_X A^{\top} + B\Sigma_Y B^{\top}\bigr).
    \]
    If $X$ and $Y$ are jointly normal with cross-covariance matrix $\Sigma_{XY} = \operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, the resulting covariance matrix generalises to $A\Sigma_X A^{\top} + B\Sigma_Y B^{\top} + A\Sigma_{XY}B^{\top} + B\Sigma_{XY}^{\top} A^{\top}$. Degenerate distributions with singular covariance matrices (including constant vectors where $\Sigma = 0$) are fully accommodated within this framework.

  

    % \item Among all continuous distributions on $\mathbb{R}^d$ with a fixed mean and fixed covariance matrix $\Sigma \succ 0$, the multivariate normal distribution $\mathcal{N}_d(\mu, \Sigma)$ uniquely maximizes differential entropy.

    \item While independence always implies zero correlation ($\Sigma_{XY} = 0$), the converse holds for Gaussian vectors \emph{if and only if} the combined vector $(X^{\top}, Y^{\top})^{\top}$ is jointly normal.
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}[Normal marginals do not make a normal vector]\label{rem:gaussiantraps}
Let $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ and let $\varepsilon$ be independent of $X$ with $\mathbb{P}(\varepsilon = 1) = \mathbb{P}(\varepsilon = -1) = \tfrac{1}{2}$. Put $Y := \varepsilon X$. Conditioning on $\varepsilon$ shows at once that $Y \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$, and
\[
\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon]\,\mathbb{E}[X^2] = 0,
\]
so $X$ and $Y$ are uncorrelated standard normal variables. They are very far from independent: $|X| = |Y|$ almost surely. Moreover, the sum $X + Y = (1+\varepsilon)X$ vanishes with probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$, hence is not normal at all.

The moral is that ``normal'' is a property of a \emph{joint law on $\mathbb{R}^d$}, not a property of a list of marginals. The pair $(X,Y)$ above has normal marginals on $\mathbb{R}$, but the vector $(X,Y)^{\top}$ is not multivariate normal in $\mathbb{R}^2$ in the sense of Example \ref{example:multivariatenormal}, and the standard linear-combination and independence properties fail for it. Whenever we invoke Gaussianity for several variables at once---as we shall constantly do for the increments of Brownian motion and for the driving noises of the multi-factor models of Section \ref{sec:othermodels}---it is strictly joint normality that is meant.
\end{remark}




















The normal distribution owes its central place to the following result, which we state in a weak form:

\begin{theorem}[Central Limit Theorem]\label{thm:clt}
    Let $\{X_n\}_{n=1}^\infty$ be independent, identically distributed (\emph{i.i.d.}) random variables  with finite mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2 > 0$. Then
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:clt}
    S_n := \frac{\sum_{k=1}^n X_k - n\mu}{\sigma \sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} \Normal(0,1).
    \end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\textbf{\textit{Proof:}} Put $Y_k := (X_k - \mu)/\sigma$, so that the $Y_k$ are i.i.d.\ with
$\E[Y_1] = 0$ and $\E[Y_1^2]=1$, and $S_n = n^{-1/2}\sum_{k=1}^n Y_k$. Write $\charf$ for the common
characteristic function of the $Y_k$.

Since $\E[Y_1^2] < \infty$, Proposition \ref{prop:charfnproperties} vi) makes $\charf$ twice
differentiable with $\charf(0)=1$, $\charf'(0) = i\,\E[Y_1] = 0$ and
$\charf''(0) = i^2\,\E[Y_1^2] = -1$. Taylor's theorem with Peano remainder therefore gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:clttaylor}
\charf(u) = 1 - \tfrac{1}{2}u^2 + o(u^2), \qquad u \to 0 .
\end{equation}
By independence and items iv) and v) of the same proposition, $\charf_{S_n}(u) = \charf(u/\sqrt n)^n$.

Fix $u \in \R$ and set $z_n := \charf(u/\sqrt n)$ and $w_n := 1 - u^2/(2n)$. Both have modulus at
most $1$ once $n \geq u^2$, the first because characteristic functions are bounded by $1$ and the
second because $0 \leq w_n \leq 1$ for such $n$. The elementary estimate
$|z^n - w^n| \leq n\,|z-w|$, valid for $|z|,|w|\leq1$ and obtained by telescoping
$z^n - w^n = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} z^{j}(z-w)w^{n-1-j}$, then yields
\[
\bigl| \charf_{S_n}(u) - w_n^{\,n} \bigr| \;\leq\; n\,\bigl|\charf(u/\sqrt n) - w_n\bigr|
\;=\; n \cdot o\!\left(\frac{u^2}{n}\right) \;\longrightarrow\; 0 ,
\]
the middle equality being \eqref{eq:clttaylor} applied at $u/\sqrt n \to 0$. Since
$w_n^{\,n} = (1-u^2/2n)^n \to e^{-u^2/2}$, we conclude that
$\charf_{S_n}(u) \to e^{-u^2/2}$ for every $u \in \R$.

The limit is continuous at the origin, so Theorem \ref{theorem:LevyContinuity} applies and
$S_n$ converges in distribution to the law with characteristic function $e^{-u^2/2}$, which is
$\Normal(0,1)$. \hfill $\blacksquare$


% \noindent Observe where the hypotheses enter. Finiteness of the variance is what licenses the
% second-order expansion \eqref{eq:clttaylor}, and it is the only place the assumption is used;
% this is the precise sense in which the limit ignores everything about the law of $X_1$ beyond its
% first two moments. Note also that the argument never assumes in advance that $e^{-u^2/2}$ is a
% characteristic function --- that it is one is supplied by Lévy's theorem, which is exactly the form
% of the statement discussed after Theorem \ref{theorem:LevyContinuity}.



\begin{theorem}[Cramér--Wold device]\label{thm:cramerwold}
Let $X_n, X$ be $\R^d$-valued random vectors. Then, the limit $X_n \xrightarrow{d} X$ holds if and only if
$a\transp X_n \xrightarrow{d} a\transp X$ for every $a \in \R^d$.
\end{theorem}

The proof is immediate from Theorem \ref{theorem:LevyContinuity}: the characteristic function of
$a\transp X_n$ evaluated at $1$ is $\charf_{X_n}(a)$, so convergence of all one-dimensional
projections is precisely pointwise convergence of $\charf_{X_n}$, and the limit is continuous at the
origin because it is a characteristic function. Combining this with the one-dimensional Central Limit
Theorem gives the form we shall use.

\begin{corollary}[Multivariate Central Limit Theorem]\label{cor:multivariateclt}
Let $\{X_n\}_{n\geq1}$ be i.i.d.\ $\R^d$-valued random vectors with mean $\mu$ and finite
covariance $\Sigma$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:multivariateclt}
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bigl(X_k - \mu\bigr) \xrightarrow{d} \Normal_d(0,\Sigma).
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\textbf{\textit{Proof:}} Fix $a \in \R^d$. The variables $a\transp X_k$ are i.i.d.\ real,
with mean $a\transp\mu$ and variance $a\transp\Sigma a$, so the one-dimensional Central Limit
Theorem \ref{thm:clt} gives $n^{-1/2}\sum_k a\transp(X_k-\mu) \xrightarrow{d} \Normal(0, a\transp\Sigma a)$. By
Example \ref{example:multivariatenormal} that limit is the law of $a\transp Z$ for
$Z \sim \Normal_d(0,\Sigma)$. Since $a$ was arbitrary, Theorem \ref{thm:cramerwold} yields
\eqref{eq:multivariateclt}. The degenerate case $a\transp\Sigma a = 0$ is included, the limit then
being the constant $0$. \hfill $\blacksquare$

For a multivariate normal vector (and, as Remark \ref{rem:gaussiantraps} showed, only then) uncorrelatedness does imply independence. Indeed, if $\Sigma$ is block-diagonal then \eqref{eq:mvncharfn} factorises as a product of the characteristic functions of the corresponding sub-vectors, which is exactly independence.

\begin{example}[Log-Normal Distribution]
A random variable \( X \) is said to follow a \textbf{log-normal distribution} with parameters \( \mu \in \R \) and \( \sigma > 0 \), denoted
\begin{equation}
X \sim \text{Log}\Normal(\mu, \sigma^2),
\end{equation}
if the logarithm of \( X \) is normally distributed, i.e.,
\begin{equation}
\log X \sim \Normal(\mu, \sigma^2).
\end{equation}
\end{example}

The probability density function of \( X \sim \text{Log}\Normal(\mu, \sigma^2) \), after a straightforward change-of-variable calculation, is
\begin{equation}
f_X(x) = \frac{1}{x \sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left( -\frac{(\log x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right), \quad x > 0.
\end{equation}

The formula $\E[X^k] = \exp(k\mu + \tfrac12 k^2\sigma^2)$ holds for every $k \in \R$,
not merely for $k \in \N$: it is obtained by writing $X^k = e^{kZ}$ with
$Z \sim \Normal(\mu,\sigma^2)$ and evaluating the Gaussian moment generating function, which is
finite for every real argument. Moments of \emph{all} real orders, negative ones included, therefore
exist and are finite. In particular $\E[X] = e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}$ and
$\Var[X] = (e^{\sigma^2}-1)e^{2\mu+\sigma^2}$. And yet the
log-normal distribution has two unpleasant analytical features.

The first is that its characteristic function admits no closed-form expression. This is a genuine inconvenience. It is precisely why the Black--Scholes model, despite its explicit pricing formula, is not amenable to the Fourier techniques that make the Heston model of the next chapter (Subsection \ref{subsec:stochvol}) tractable, but this is not a pathology. Note that $\charf_X$ is defined and bounded by $1$ for every \emph{real} $u$, as it is for any random variable; what fails is the \emph{moment generating function} $\E[e^{uX}]$, which is infinite for every $u > 0$. The two objects are easily confused and we shall keep them apart.

 Despite having moments of all orders, \textbf{the log-normal distribution is not determined by them}: there is an explicit one-parameter family of mutually distinct densities on $(0,\infty)$ sharing every moment with it, exhibited by Heyde \cite{heyde1963} and reproduced in Appendix \ref{app:heyde}. It is worth being precise about what does \emph{not} fail here. Lévy's inversion formula \eqref{levyInversionFormula} holds without exception, and the characteristic function does determine the log-normal law uniquely. What fails is the passage from the \emph{moment sequence} to the characteristic function.

% The logical status of that failure deserves care, because it is routinely overstated. Carleman's condition $\sum_k m_{2k}^{-1/2k} = \infty$ is \emph{sufficient} for a law to be determined by its moments, not necessary. The log-normal moments grow as $e^{k^2\sigma^2/2}$, which is fast enough to violate the condition --- but a violated sufficient condition establishes only that the criterion is silent, never that the conclusion is false. What establishes indeterminacy here is nothing to do with growth rates: it is Heyde's explicit exhibition of a one-parameter family of distinct densities sharing the moment sequence. The growth rate tells us why no elementary criterion could have saved us; the construction tells us that nothing could.

The lesson is one we shall meet again in Chapter \ref{ch:roughpaths}: knowing every moment of an object is strictly weaker than knowing the object, and the signature transform will be interesting precisely because, for paths, the analogous sufficient condition is \emph{satisfied} rather than violated, so that the corresponding statement can be made to go the right way.

We present two more elementary processes.

\begin{example}[Random walk and Poisson process]\label{example:randomwalk}


i) Let $\{\xi_n\}$ be i.i.d.\ with $\PP(\xi_n = 1) = \PP(\xi_n=-1)=\tfrac12$. The
\textbf{simple symmetric random walk} is $X_0 := 0$, $X_n := \sum_{k=1}^n \xi_k$: a discrete-time,
integer-valued process with stationary and independent increments. Since
$\charf_\xi(u) = \cos(u)$, independence gives $\charf_{X_n}(u) = \cos^n(u)$.

ii) Fix $\lambda>0$. A process $N = \{N_t\}_{t\geq0}$ with values in $\N_0$ is a
\textbf{Poisson process with rate $\lambda$} if $N_0 = 0$ almost surely, $N$ has independent
increments, and
\begin{equation}
\PP(N_{t+s}-N_s = k) = \frac{(\lambda t)^k}{k!}e^{-\lambda t}, \qquad k \in \N_0,\ s,t\geq0 .
\end{equation}
Its paths are piecewise constant and non-decreasing, with unit jumps at random times;
$\E[N_t] = \Var[N_t] = \lambda t$ and
$\charf_{N_t}(u) = e^{\lambda t(e^{iu}-1)}$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Markov Processes}\label{subsec:markovprocesses}

Markov processes are characterised by a specific form of \emph{memorylessness}. They are particularly suited to describe the evolution of systems where the future depends only on the present state and not on the past history. Some of these processes arise as \textit{diffusions} and in that case, a lot can be said about the distributions.

\begin{definition}[Markov Process]
Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \PP)\) be a probability space, and let \(X = \{X_t\}_{t \in \Tset}\) be a stochastic process taking values in a measurable space \((E, \mathcal{E})\). We say that \(X\) is a \textbf{Markov process} if for every $s < t$ and every measurable set $A \in \mathcal{E}$,
\begin{equation}
\PP(X_t \in A \mid \mathcal{F}_s) = \PP(X_t \in A \mid X_s) \quad \text{almost surely},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{F}_s = \sigma(X_r : r \leq s)$ is the $\sigma$-algebra generated by the history of the process up to time $s$. More generally, given a filtration $\Filt$ to which $X$ is adapted, $X$ is said to be Markov \emph{with respect to $\Filt$} if the same identity holds with $\mathcal{F}_s$ that filtration; this is the form asserted for Brownian motion in Proposition \ref{prop:bm-properties}(d), and it is strictly stronger, since $\Filt$ may carry more information than $X$ alone.
\end{definition}

The \textbf{transition probability} of a Markov process is $\PP(X_t \in A \mid X_s = x)$; in order to integrate against the measure it defines we write $P_{s,x}(t,dy) = \PP(X_t \in dy \mid X_s = x).$

There is a rather sharp distinction between finite-state Markov processes and those with infinitely many states. In the finite case, the theory is built around transition matrices: the probability of moving from one state to another in a single step is recorded in a matrix $P_t$, so that the evolution is the product $\prod_{i=1}^n P_i$, and long-run behaviour can be analysed by classical linear-algebraic tools, especially in the stationary case, that is, when the transition probability is independent of $t$.

In the infinite-state setting the dynamics are instead described by operators acting on spaces of functions or measures, so that the study of these processes relies on functional analysis and differential equations as much as on probability. That apparatus, the transition semigroup $(P_t)_{t\geq0}$, its infinitesimal generator, and the Kolmogorov equations they satisfy is developed in Subsection \ref{section:itoDiff}, once diffusions are available to make it concrete.

\subsection{Martingales, semimartingales and local martingales}\label{subsec:martingales}

A martingale is a process whose expected future value, conditional on everything observed so far, is its present value: a fair game. Martingales are central to the study of stochastic processes, and two weakenings of the notion are needed alongside it.

\begin{definition}[Martingale] 
    Let $X_t$ be a stochastic process, adapted to a given filtration $\mathcal{F}_t$. We say it is a \textbf{martingale} provided:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $\E[|X_t|]<\infty$ for all $t.$
        \item $X_s = \E[X_t | \mathcal{F}_s]$ for $s<t.$
    \end{itemize}
    We say that $X_t$ is a \textbf{supermartingale} if the second condition is replaced by $X_s  \geq \E[X_t | \mathcal{F}_s]$ and a \textbf{submartingale} if $X_s\leq \E[X_t | \mathcal{F}_s].$\footnote{The Poisson process of Example \ref{example:randomwalk} is not a martingale, being increasing; the \emph{compensated} process $\Nt_t - \lambda t$ is.}
\end{definition}

The following result will be useful when constructing stochastic processes from Itô integrals. Notice that if $X_t$ is a martingale, then by Jensen's inequality (Proposition \ref{prop:condjensenl2}, item i)) $X_t^2$ is a submartingale.
 
\begin{proposition}[Doob's submartingale inequality]\label{prop:doobmaximal}
    Let $Z = \{Z_t\}_{t\in[0,T]}$ be a right-continuous submartingale. Then, for any $\varepsilon > 0$,
    $$
    \PP\Bigl(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T} Z_t \geq \varepsilon\Bigr) \leq \frac{\E[\max\{Z_T,0\}]}{\varepsilon}.
    $$
    In particular, for a right-continuous martingale $X$, $\PP(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T} |X_t| \geq \varepsilon) \leq \E[|X_T|]/\varepsilon.$
\end{proposition}
\textbf{\textit{Proof:}} The argument is given in Appendix \ref{app:proof:doobmaximal}; see also \cite[Thm.\ 4.5.1]{kuo2006}. \hfill $\blacksquare$

The bound is in terms of the \emph{terminal} value $X_T$, not of a running $X_t$; this is the whole content of the inequality, since it converts information at a single time into control of the entire path. For the $L^2$ theory of the Itô integral we shall need the following stronger, $L^p$ form, which is proved by integrating the tail estimate above.

\begin{proposition}[Doob's $L^p$ maximal inequality]\label{prop:dooblp}
Let $X$ be a right-continuous martingale and let $p > 1$. Then
\[
\E\Bigl[\sup_{0 \leq t \leq T}|X_t|^p\Bigr] \leq \left(\frac{p}{p-1}\right)^{p} \E\bigl[|X_T|^p\bigr].
\]
In particular, for $p=2$, $\E\bigl[\sup_{t\leq T}|X_t|^2\bigr] \leq 4\,\E[|X_T|^2]$. \hfill $\square$
\end{proposition}

The case $p=2$ is the one we shall use repeatedly. It says that for a square-integrable martingale, controlling the terminal second moment controls the second moment of the whole trajectory --- which is exactly what is needed to upgrade convergence of the Itô integral in $L^2(\Omega)$, at each fixed time, to uniform convergence along a subsequence, and hence to continuity of sample paths (Theorem \ref{thm:itocontinuity}).


The martingale property is stated at deterministic times, but
localisation in Section \ref{sec:stochasticcalculus} involves evaluating processes at \emph{random} times. For this 
to make sense we must first say what information is available at a stopping time.

\begin{definition}[The $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{F}_\tau$]\label{def:Ftau}
Let $\tau$ be a stopping time with respect to $\Filt$. The $\sigma$-algebra of events observable up 
to $\tau$ is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{F}_\tau := \bigl\{ A \in \mathcal{F} \;:\; A \cap \{\tau \leq t\} \in \mathcal{F}_t \ \text{ for every } t \in [0,T] \bigr\}.
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

The definition reads oddly at first but says something simple: an event belongs to $\mathcal{F}_\tau$ 
if, whenever $\tau$ has occurred by time $t$, an observer at time $t$ can already tell whether the 
event happened. One checks readily that $\mathcal{F}_\tau$ is indeed a $\sigma$-algebra, that 
$\tau$ is $\mathcal{F}_\tau$-measurable, that $\mathcal{F}_\tau = \mathcal{F}_t$ when 
$\tau \equiv t$ is constant, and that $\varsigma \leq \tau$ implies
$\mathcal{F}_\varsigma \subseteq \mathcal{F}_\tau$ for a second stopping time $\varsigma$ --- the
letter $\sigma$ being reserved for diffusion coefficients throughout. If $X$ is right-continuous and adapted, then
$X_\tau$ is $\mathcal{F}_\tau$-measurable on $\{\tau < \infty\}$.

\begin{theorem}[Doob's optional stopping theorem]\label{thm:optionalstopping}
\emph{For this theorem only, we work on the infinite horizon:} let $X = \{X_t\}_{t\geq0}$ be a 
right-continuous martingale with respect to a filtration $(\mathcal{F}_t)_{t\geq0}$, and let 
$\tau_1 \leq \tau_2$ be stopping times with values in $[0,\infty]$. If any one of the following 
holds --- 
\begin{itemize}
    \item[i)] $\tau_2$ is bounded, i.e.\ there is $c<\infty$ with $\tau_2 \leq c$ almost surely;
    \item[ii)] $X$ is uniformly integrable (Definition \ref{def:ui}); or
    \item[iii)] there exists $Y \in L^1(\Omega)$ with $|X_{t}| \leq Y$ for all $t$,
\end{itemize}
--- then $X_{\tau_1}$ and $X_{\tau_2}$ are integrable and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optionalstopping}
\E\bigl[ X_{\tau_2} \mid \mathcal{F}_{\tau_1} \bigr] = X_{\tau_1} \qquad \text{almost surely.}
\end{equation}
In particular, taking $\tau_1 \equiv 0$, $\E[X_{\tau_2}] = \E[X_0]$. The corresponding statements
with $=$ replaced by $\geq$ or $\leq$ hold for sub- and supermartingales respectively.
\hfill $\square$
\end{theorem}

Equation \eqref{eq:optionalstopping} is the assertion that a martingale remains a martingale when
observed along a random clock. One word first on how it is to be read, since the stopping times are
allowed the value $+\infty$ and $X_\infty$ has not been defined. Under i) the question does not
arise, both times being bounded by $c$. Under ii) or iii) the martingale convergence theorem
supplies a random variable $X_\infty \in L^1(\Omega)$ with $X_t \to X_\infty$ almost surely and in
$L^1$, and $X_{\tau_i}$ is to be read as $X_\infty$ on $\{\tau_i = \infty\}$; with that reading the
statement is exact, and it is the reading intended throughout.

Two remarks on the hypotheses before we use the theorem. They are not
independent: iii) implies ii), since a family dominated by a single integrable variable is uniformly
integrable, so iii) is recorded only because it is the form in which the hypothesis is usually
easiest to check. More importantly, under the finite-horizon convention of Definition
\ref{stoppingTime} --- which is in force everywhere else in this dissertation, and under which every
stopping time satisfies $\tau_2 \leq T$ --- hypothesis i) holds automatically, and the theorem
applies unconditionally. The hypotheses therefore do work only on an infinite horizon, and that is
why the theorem has been stated there.

It is worth being explicit about what the three hypotheses have in common, since they can look like
an arbitrary list. The proof passes to the limit in the discrete-time identity
$\E[X_{\tau_2 \wedge n} \mid \mathcal{F}_{\tau_1 \wedge n}] = X_{\tau_1 \wedge n}$, which holds with
no hypothesis at all, and the only thing that can go wrong is that the limit fails to commute with
the expectation. Each of i)--iii) is a way of licensing that exchange: i) makes the truncation
vacuous for $n \geq c$, iii) is dominated convergence, and ii) is the exact condition under which
$L^1$ convergence follows from convergence in probability. Nothing else is being assumed.

Two consequences will be used repeatedly. First, applying case i) to the bounded pair
$t\wedge\tau \leq s\wedge\tau$, for a fixed stopping time $\tau$ and deterministic $t \leq s$, shows
that the \emph{stopped} process $X^{\tau} = \{X_{t\wedge\tau}\}_{t\geq0}$ is again a martingale:
stopping never destroys the martingale property, and only evaluation at an unbounded random time
can. This is the mechanism behind localisation, and Definition \ref{def:localmartingale} below is
built on it. Second, the sub- and supermartingale forms are the ones that carry the financial
content: applied to a discounted option payoff in Subsection \ref{subsec:american} of the next
chapter, the submartingale inequality is precisely the statement that early exercise cannot pay.

That they do genuine work is best seen from an example, and the example is one we shall have
occasion to recall in Chapter \ref{ch:finance}. Let $W$ be a Brownian motion, let $a>0$, and let
\[
\tau_a = \inf\{t \geq 0 : W_t = a\} .
\]
This stopping time is finite almost surely: by the reflection principle proved below (Theorem
\ref{thm:reflection}), $\PP(\tau_a \leq t) = \PP(|W_t| > a)$, and $|W_t|$ has the law of
$\sqrt{t}\,|Z|$ with $Z \sim \Normal(0,1)$, so the right-hand side tends to $1$ as $t \to \infty$.
But the path sits at the level $a$ at the moment it stops, so $W_{\tau_a} = a$ identically, and
therefore
\[
\E\bigl[W_{\tau_a}\bigr] = a \;\neq\; 0 \;=\; \E[W_0] .
\]
Equation \eqref{eq:optionalstopping} fails outright. Nothing is wrong with the theorem: $\tau_a$ is
unbounded, so i) does not apply, and the stopped family $\{W_{t\wedge\tau_a}\}_{t\geq0}$ is not
uniformly integrable, so neither does ii). The conclusion is genuinely lost without a hypothesis of
this kind --- and this is precisely the phenomenon that a finite horizon $[0,T]$ conceals, since
there every stopping time is bounded by $T$ and i) holds for free.

The example also has an exact financial reading, which is the reason for going to the trouble. A
strategy that waits however long is needed in order to be one unit ahead does win with certainty. It
is not an arbitrage --- and the theory of Chapter \ref{ch:finance} had better not classify it as one
--- only because executing it may demand unbounded losses along the way. Excluding exactly such
strategies is the whole purpose of the admissibility constraint of Definition \ref{def:admissible}.

\noindent
The notion of a martingale is often too restrictive, particularly when considering processes such as stochastic exponentials or certain diffusion models. A relaxation is therefore introduced.

\begin{definition}[Local Martingale]\label{def:localmartingale}
A process $X = \{X_t\}_{t \in [0,T]}$ is a \textbf{local martingale} if there exists an increasing sequence of stopping times $(\tau_n)_{n \geq 1}$ with $\tau_n \uparrow T$ almost surely and $\PP(\tau_n = T) \to 1$, such that each stopped process $X^{\tau_n}_t := X_{t \wedge \tau_n}$ is a martingale. The second requirement is what pins down $X_T$: without it the $\tau_n$ could all stay strictly below $T$ and the terminal value would be unconstrained. On an infinite horizon the condition reads $\tau_n \uparrow \infty$; we shall use the finite-horizon form, which is the one that appears in the proof of Theorem \ref{thm:sdeexistence}.
\end{definition}

% The idea is that $X$ behaves like a martingale, but only up to random horizons. An analogy could be drawn from ODEs: there are solutions that explode to infinity in finite time, and those that can be extended to all times. Here, the integrability condition can be somewhat replaced if we work with sequences of stopping times. This mathematical object deserves its own study but lies beyond the scope of this dissertation.

Before introducing the last class of processes, we record two ways of measuring how much a function
moves. Both are needed to state the definition that follows, and both will be with us for the rest
of the chapter.

\begin{definition}[Total variation]\label{def:totalvariation}
The \textbf{total variation} of $f : [0,T]\to\R$ on $[0,t]$ is
\begin{equation}
V_1(f)_t := \sup_{\mathcal{P}} \sum_{k=1}^n \bigl| f(t_k) - f(t_{k-1}) \bigr|,
\end{equation}
the supremum being over all partitions $\mathcal{P} = \{0=t_0<\cdots<t_n=t\}$ of $[0,t]$. If
$V_1(f)_t < \infty$ we say $f$ has \textbf{bounded variation} on $[0,t]$.
\end{definition}

Bounded variation is exactly the condition under which the Riemann--Stieltjes integral
$\int_0^t g\,df$ is defined for every continuous $g$; equivalently, it is the condition under
which $f$ induces a signed measure $df$. It is therefore the natural first thing to ask of a
prospective integrator.

\begin{definition}[Quadratic Variation] \label{def:quadratic-variation}
Let \(f : [0,T] \to \R\) and let \(\mathcal{P} = \{0=t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_n=t\}\) be a partition of
\([0,t]\). Write
\begin{equation}
\lVert \mathcal{P} \rVert = \max_{1 \leq k \leq n} (t_k - t_{k-1}).
\end{equation}
If the limit
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qvdef}
[f]_t := \lim_{\lVert \mathcal{P} \rVert \to 0}
\sum_{k=1}^n \big( f(t_k) - f(t_{k-1}) \big)^2
\end{equation}
exists, it is called the \textbf{quadratic variation} of \(f\) on \([0,t]\). Similarly, for \(g : [0,T] \to \R\) we define the \textbf{covariation} of \(f\) and \(g\) on \([0,t]\) as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:covdef}
[f,g]_t := \lim_{\lVert \mathcal{P} \rVert \to 0}
\sum_{k=1}^n \big( f(t_k) - f(t_{k-1}) \big) \big( g(t_k) - g(t_{k-1}) \big)
\end{equation}
provided this limit exists, so that $[f]_t = [f,f]_t$.
\end{definition}

Two cautions are in order. First, the limit in \eqref{eq:qvdef} need not exist for an arbitrary $f$. Second, for a \emph{stochastic} process the sums in \eqref{eq:qvdef} are random variables, and the limit must be specified as a limit of random variables. We shall always understand it in $L^2(\Omega)$, which for our purposes is both the easiest to obtain and strong enough to imply convergence in probability, and hence almost sure convergence along a subsequence. 


 If $f$ is continuously differentiable, then $|f(t_k)-f(t_{k-1})| \leq C\,(t_k - t_{k-1})$ and the sum is bounded by $C^2 \lVert\mathcal{P}\rVert \, t \to 0$: smooth functions have zero quadratic variation. The same computation applies to any continuous function of bounded variation. A non-zero quadratic variation is therefore a certificate of irregularity, and Proposition \ref{prop:bm-properties}(e) below will assert that Brownian motion carries exactly such a certificate. Quadratic variation acts as a measure of the ``roughness'' of the path. We will see that, for Brownian Motion, $[W]_t = t$. Should we then write $dW_t \approx \sqrt{dt}$? As a mnemonic, yes: this is exactly the entry $dW_t\,dW_t = dt$ of the It\^o table of Table \ref{tab:ItoTableScalar}, and Subsection \ref{subsec:itointegral} onwards is the disciplined version of the guess.

There is a second notion of quadratic variation, obtained not by refining partitions but by
subtracting from $M^2$ its martingale part, and it is the one the theory will actually run on. It is
supplied by the following classical decomposition, whose statement needs one word we have not yet
defined. Writing $\mathbb{L}$ for the adapted processes with almost all sample paths
left-continuous, the \textbf{predictable} $\sigma$-algebra is the smallest $\sigma$-algebra on
$[0,T]\times\Omega$ making every element of $\mathbb{L}$ measurable, and a process is predictable if
it is measurable with respect to it.

\begin{theorem}[Doob--Meyer decomposition]\label{thm:doobmeyer}
Let $M = \{M_t\}_{t\in[0,T]}$ be a càdlàg, square-integrable martingale. Then $M^2$ admits a
unique decomposition
\begin{equation}\label{eq:compensatorannounce}
M_t^2 = R_t + \qv{M}_t ,
\end{equation}
in which $R$ is a càdlàg martingale and $\qv{M}$ is predictable, right-continuous and
increasing, with $\qv{M}_0 = 0$ and $\E\bigl[\qv{M}_t\bigr] < \infty$ for every $t \in [0,T]$.
\qedopen
\end{theorem}

The process $\qv{M}$ is called the \textbf{compensator} of $M^2$, or the \emph{predictable quadratic
variation} of $M$. The theorem holds in a considerably more general form for submartingales; but if
$M$ is a square-integrable martingale then $M^2$ is a submartingale by Jensen's inequality
(Proposition \ref{prop:condjensenl2}, item i)), and the version above is all we shall require. For a
proof see \cite{kuo2006} or \cite{shreve2004}.

Predictability is what makes the decomposition \emph{unique}, and this is the one place where we
need the word; it is worth saying at once why it will cost us nothing. When the integrator has
continuous sample paths --- as Brownian motion does, and as every model in
Part \ref{part:part1} does --- predictable and merely adapted integrands give rise to the same
stochastic integrals, which is why the construction of Subsection \ref{subsec:itointegral} will be
able to proceed without the notion. The distinction becomes essential the moment the integrator is
allowed to jump, and for a reason about information rather than technique: if the integrand may
``see'' a jump as it happens, one can bet on the jump at the instant it occurs, and the resulting
integral fails to be a martingale. Predictability forbids exactly this, and its financial reading is
that one may not trade on information one does not yet have. This is not idle for genuinely
discontinuous underlyings --- electricity spot prices, several commodities, and credit-sensitive
instruments where default is by nature a jump --- but we shall not need it again.

Two notions of quadratic variation are now in play: the pathwise $[M]$ of Definition
\ref{def:quadratic-variation}, defined by refining partitions, and the predictable $\qv{M}$ just
obtained, defined by a decomposition. For the processes of this dissertation they agree.  

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:qvcoincide}
Let $M$ be a continuous square-integrable martingale. Then the pathwise quadratic variation 
$[M]_t$ of Definition \ref{def:quadratic-variation} exists as a limit in $L^2(\Omega)$ and 
coincides, up to indistinguishability, with the compensator $\langle M \rangle_t$ of Theorem 
\ref{thm:doobmeyer}. Furthermore, the polarised quantity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:polarisation}
\langle M, N \rangle_t := \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl( \langle M+N \rangle_t - \langle M \rangle_t - \langle N \rangle_t \bigr)
= \lim_{\lVert\mathcal{P}\rVert\to0} \sum_k \bigl(M_{t_k}-M_{t_{k-1}}\bigr)\bigl(N_{t_k}-N_{t_{k-1}}\bigr)
\end{equation}
defines the \textbf{quadratic covariation} of two continuous square-integrable martingales, is 
bilinear and symmetric, and satisfies the Kunita--Watanabe inequality
\begin{equation}
\int_0^t \bigl| d\langle M,N\rangle_s \bigr| \;\leq\; \sqrt{\langle M\rangle_t}\,\sqrt{\langle N\rangle_t}
\qquad \text{almost surely.}
\end{equation}
\qedopen
\end{proposition}

The distinction between $[M]$ and $\langle M\rangle$ is genuine for processes with jumps, where the
two differ by the accumulated squared jumps;\footnote{For the compensated Poisson process
$M_t = \Nt_t - \lambda t$ one has $[M]_t = \Nt_t$, which is random, while $\langle M\rangle_t = \lambda t$
is deterministic: every jump of $\Nt$ has size $1$, so each contributes $1$ to $[M]$.} for the continuous processes of this dissertation it
is empty, and we shall write $\langle M \rangle$ throughout. Quadratic covariation is what gives 
meaning to the off-diagonal entries $\rho_{ij}\,dt$ of the multiplication table in Subsection 
\ref{subsec:multidimensional}, and it is the object that the correlation parameter of a stochastic 
volatility model actually controls.




\noindent
Finally, there is another related concept, broad enough to serve as a class of \textit{good integrators}. In particular, it extends the role played by Brownian motion as a canonical integrator, while retaining enough structure to allow a general theory of stochastic calculus.

\begin{definition}[Semimartingale]\label{def:semimartingale}
A process $X$ is called a \textbf{semimartingale} if it can be decomposed as
\[
X_t = M_t + A_t,
\]
where $M_t$ is a càdlàg local martingale and $A_t$ is adapted, \textit{continue à droite, avec limites à gauche} (càdlàg), of locally bounded variation, with $M_0 = X_0$ and $A_0 = 0$.
\end{definition}

This last definition is the correct level of generality for stochastic integration, and it is 
worth understanding why the decomposition takes the shape it does. The two summands are exactly the 
two mechanisms by which an integrator can be usable: $A$ has bounded variation, so 
$\int g\,dA$ exists pathwise as a Riemann--Stieltjes integral in the classical sense; and $M$ is a 
local martingale, so $\int g\,dM$ exists in the Itô sense. The last clause deserves a caveat, since 
the construction of Section \ref{sec:stochasticcalculus} is carried out for $M = W$ specifically and 
uses $\E[(W_{t_i}-W_{t_{i-1}})^2] = t_i - t_{i-1}$ at its one essential step, the isometry of 
Theorem \ref{thm:itoisometry}. For a general continuous local martingale the same construction goes 
through with $\int_0^T \E[f^2]\,dt$ replaced throughout by 
$\E\int_0^T f^2\,d\qv{M}_t$, the compensator of \eqref{eq:compensatorannounce}; localisation then
removes the integrability hypotheses. We do not carry this out, and shall integrate only against
Brownian motion.


Granting it, a semimartingale is precisely a process that can be integrated against by splitting the 
work between deterministic analysis and probability, and the Bichteler--Dellacherie theorem makes 
the converse precise: semimartingales are, in a suitable topological sense, the \emph{only} 
processes admitting a reasonable theory of stochastic integration; see \cite{protter2005}.

\subsection{Brownian Motion}\label{subsec:brownianmotion}

Brownian motion is a fundamental stochastic process whose importance has grown 
throughout many areas of mathematics. It is rich enough to generate a new class 
of \textit{stochastic differential equations} and to address numerous applied problems 
involving randomness, yet simple enough to allow explicit calculations. 
Its properties also enable efficient simulation, making it a cornerstone of Monte Carlo 
methods across disciplines.

\begin{definition}[Brownian Motion] \label{def:Brownian-motion}
A stochastic process \(W : \R^+ \times \Omega \to \R\) is called a 
\textbf{Brownian motion} (or \textbf{Wiener process}) if the following conditions hold:
\begin{itemize}
    \item[i)] \begin{equation}
        \PP(W_0 = 0) = 1.
    \end{equation}
    \item[ii)] \begin{equation}
        \PP\big( \{ \omega : t \mapsto W_t(\omega) 
        \text{ is continuous} \} \big) = 1.
    \end{equation}
    \item[iii)]  For \(0 \leq s < t\), 
    \begin{equation}
        W_t - W_s \sim \Normal(0, t-s)
    \end{equation}
    so that $W_t \sim \Normal(0, t)$.
    \item[iv)] The process has independent increments: for 
    \(0 \leq t_1 < t_2 < \dots < t_n\), the random variables
    \begin{equation}
        \{ W_{t_1}, \ W_{t_2}-W_{t_1}, \ W_{t_3}-W_{t_2}, \ \dots, \ W_{t_n}-W_{t_{n-1}} \}
    \end{equation}
    are independent.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}





\begin{proposition} \label{prop:bm-properties}
Let \((W_t)_{t \geq 0}\) be a Brownian motion. Then:
\begin{itemize}
    \item[a)] For all 
    \(s,t \geq 0\),
    \begin{equation}
        \E[W_s W_t] = \min\{s,t\}.
    \end{equation}
    \item[b)] For \(t_0 \geq 0\), the process
    \begin{equation}
        Y(t) = W(t+t_0) - W(t_0)
    \end{equation}
    is also a Brownian motion.
    \item[c)] For any \(\lambda > 0\), the process
    \begin{equation}
        Z(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}} W(\lambda t)
    \end{equation}
    is also a Brownian motion.
    \item[d)] Brownian motion is both a Markov process and a martingale. 
    \item[e)] The quadratic variation of \(W\) over \([0,t]\) exists and equals
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:bmqv}
        [W]_t = t \qquad \text{the limit being taken in } L^2(\Omega).
    \end{equation}
    \item[f)] The process $W_t^2 - t$ is a martingale.
\end{itemize}
Properties b) and c) are called \emph{translation invariance} 
and \emph{scaling invariance}, respectively.
\end{proposition}

\textbf{\textit{Proof:}} a) By i) we have $W_t = W_t - W_0$ almost surely, so $W_t \sim \Normal(0,t)$
by iii). For $s < t$, write $W_t = (W_t - W_s) + W_s$ and use that $W_t - W_s$ is independent of
$W_s$ with zero mean:
\[
\E[W_sW_t] = \E\bigl[W_s(W_t-W_s)\bigr] + \E[W_s^2] = 0 + s = \min\{s,t\}.
\]

b) $Y_0 = 0$ and the paths of $Y$ are continuous, since those of $W$ are. For $s<t$ we have
$Y_t - Y_s = W_{t+t_0} - W_{s+t_0} \sim \Normal(0,t-s)$, and increments of $Y$ over disjoint
intervals are increments of $W$ over disjoint intervals, hence independent.

c) Again $Z_0=0$ and the paths are continuous. For $s<t$,
$Z_t - Z_s = \lambda^{-1/2}(W_{\lambda t} - W_{\lambda s})$ is normal with mean $0$ and variance
$\lambda^{-1}(\lambda t - \lambda s) = t-s$; independence of increments is inherited from $W$ because
$s \mapsto \lambda s$ preserves disjointness of intervals.

d) Integrability is clear from a). For $s<t$, using independence of $W_t-W_s$ from $\mathcal{F}_s$
and $\mathcal{F}_s$-measurability of $W_s$,
\[
\E[W_t \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = \E[W_t - W_s \mid \mathcal{F}_s] + \E[W_s\mid\mathcal{F}_s]
= \E[W_t-W_s] + W_s = W_s,
\]
so $W$ is a martingale. The same independence, applied this time to
$\PP(W_t \in A \mid \mathcal{F}_s) = \PP\bigl(W_s + (W_t-W_s) \in A \mid \mathcal{F}_s\bigr)$,
yields $\PP(W_t \in A \mid \mathcal{F}_s) = g(W_s)$ with
$g(x) = \PP\bigl(x + \Normal(0,t-s) \in A\bigr)$: the conditional law of $W_t$ given
$\mathcal{F}_s$ depends on $\omega$ only through $W_s$, which is the Markov property.

e) Let $\mathcal{P}=\{0=t_0<\cdots<t_n=t\}$ and set
$X_k = (W_{t_k}-W_{t_{k-1}})^2 - (t_k - t_{k-1})$. The $X_k$ are independent with
$\E[X_k]=0$, and since $\E[Z^4]=3\sigma^4$ for $Z\sim\Normal(0,\sigma^2)$,
\[
\E[X_k^2] = 3(t_k-t_{k-1})^2 - 2(t_k-t_{k-1})^2 + (t_k-t_{k-1})^2 = 2(t_k-t_{k-1})^2 .
\]
Hence, by independence and orthogonality,
\[
\E\Bigl[\Bigl(\sum_k (W_{t_k}-W_{t_{k-1}})^2 - t\Bigr)^{\!2}\Bigr]
= \sum_k \E[X_k^2] = 2\sum_k (t_k-t_{k-1})^2 \leq 2\lVert\mathcal{P}\rVert\,t \longrightarrow 0
\]
as $\lVert\mathcal{P}\rVert \to 0$, which is \eqref{eq:bmqv}. See also \cite[Thm.\ 4.1.2]{kuo2006}.

f) Integrability follows from a). For $s<t$, expanding $W_t^2 = (W_t-W_s)^2 + 2W_s(W_t-W_s) + W_s^2$
and conditioning,
\[
\E[W_t^2 \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = (t-s) + 2W_s\cdot 0 + W_s^2,
\]
so $\E[W_t^2 - t \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = W_s^2 - s$. \hfill $\blacksquare$

\medskip

One might wonder whether such a process actually exists, given the strong 
set of requirements in Definition~\ref{def:Brownian-motion}. A full construction would 
take us too far, but it would be unsatisfying to leave the matter entirely unaddressed.

The construction proceeds in two stages. The 
first one produces a process with the right finite-dimensional distributions and says nothing 
whatever about its paths; the second repairs the paths without disturbing the distributions.

The first stage is the Daniell--Kolmogorov extension theorem. Conditions iii) and iv) of Definition 
\ref{def:Brownian-motion} prescribe, for every finite set of times $t_1 < \cdots < t_n$, the joint 
law of $(W_{t_1},\dots,W_{t_n})$: it is the centred Gaussian law on $\R^n$ with covariance 
$\min\{t_i,t_j\}$, which is positive semi-definite and therefore legitimate. These laws are 
\emph{consistent}, in the sense that the law prescribed for a larger set of times marginalises to 
the law prescribed for a smaller one. The 
extension theorem then asserts that any consistent family of finite-dimensional distributions is 
realised by some process on the product space $\R^{[0,\infty)}$; see 
\cite[Thm.\ 2.2.2]{karatzasshreve1991} or \cite[Ch.\ 8]{durrett2019}.

What this stage cannot deliver is condition ii). The set of continuous paths is not measurable in 
the product $\sigma$-algebra since membership in it depends on uncountably many coordinates at once, 
so continuity is invisible to the finite-dimensional data and must be obtained separately. That is 
the second stage, and it does so from a moment condition alone.

\begin{theorem}[Kolmogorov's continuity criterion]\label{thm:kolmogorovcontinuity}
Let $X = \{X_t\}_{t\in[0,T]}$ be a stochastic process for which there exist constants 
$\alpha, \beta, C > 0$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kolmogorovmoment}
\E\bigl[\,|X_t - X_s|^{\alpha}\,\bigr] \leq C\,|t-s|^{1+\beta}, \qquad s,t \in [0,T].
\end{equation}
Then $X$ admits a continuous modification $\tilde{X}$. Moreover, the paths of $\tilde{X}$ are
almost surely locally $\gamma$-Hölder continuous for every $\gamma \in (0,\beta/\alpha)$; see
\cite[Thm.\ 2.2.8]{karatzasshreve1991}.
\hfill $\square$
\end{theorem}

Applied to Brownian motion the criterion is immediate. Since $W_t - W_s \sim \Normal(0,t-s)$, 
the Gaussian moments give $\E[|W_t-W_s|^{2m}] = c_m |t-s|^{m}$ for every $m \in \N$, 
so \eqref{eq:kolmogorovmoment} holds with $\alpha = 2m$ and $\beta = m-1$. A continuous 
modification therefore exists, which is what was needed; and the Hölder conclusion yields 
exponents $\gamma < (m-1)/2m$, which as $m \to \infty$ approach $1/2$ from below but never reach 
it. That the supremum is exactly $1/2$, and the fact that it is not attained, is the subject of the next 
subsection.


\begin{remark}[Modification versus indistinguishability]\label{rem:modification}
What Theorem \ref{thm:kolmogorovcontinuity} produces is a process agreeing with $X$ at each fixed
time almost surely, not one agreeing with $X$ along whole paths, and the distinction has a name.
Two processes $X$ and $Y$ on the same space are \textbf{modifications} of each other if
$\PP(X_t = Y_t) = 1$ for every fixed $t$, and \textbf{indistinguishable} if
$\PP(X_t = Y_t \text{ for all } t) = 1$. The second is strictly stronger: in the first the
exceptional null set is allowed to depend on $t$, and an uncountable union of null sets need not be
null. The two notions do coincide when both processes have right-continuous paths, which is the
situation we shall almost always be in --- but the coincidence is a small theorem, not a definition,
and we shall be careful to say which of the two we mean.  As is customary, we shall always work with the continuous modification and
cease to mention the distinction.
\end{remark}

The two stages now fit together. Daniell--Kolmogorov supplies a process whose finite-dimensional
distributions are the prescribed Gaussian ones, hence a process satisfying conditions i), iii) and
iv) of Definition \ref{def:Brownian-motion} and saying nothing about ii); Kolmogorov's criterion
replaces it by a modification with continuous paths, which by Remark \ref{rem:modification} carries
the same finite-dimensional distributions and therefore retains i), iii) and iv) while acquiring
ii). Since the criterion is stated on a compact interval, one applies it on each $[0,n]$ and patches,
the modifications so obtained agreeing almost surely on overlaps. Existence is settled. We now give a more constructive approach.



Turning back to Example
\ref{example:randomwalk} on random walk, the following result shows how a piecewise linear interpolation of a rescaled random walk converges to Brownian motion.

\begin{theorem}[Donsker's invariance principle]\label{thm:donsker}
Let $\{\xi_k\}_{k\geq1}$ be i.i.d.\ with $\E[\xi_1]=0$ and $\Var[\xi_1]=1$, and let 
$X_n = \sum_{k=1}^n \xi_k$ be the associated random walk. Define the rescaled, linearly 
interpolated process
\begin{equation}\label{eq:donskerscaling}
W^{(n)}_t := \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigl( X_{\lfloor nt \rfloor} + (nt - \lfloor nt \rfloor)\,\xi_{\lfloor nt\rfloor +1}\Bigr), \qquad t \in [0,T].
\end{equation}
Then $W^{(n)} \xrightarrow{d} W$ as $n \to \infty$, where the convergence is in distribution on the 
space $C[0,T]$ of continuous functions with the uniform topology, and $W$ is a Brownian motion. 
\hfill $\square$
\end{theorem}

This gives a constructive route to Brownian motion. The scaling in \eqref{eq:donskerscaling} is the whole content of the theorem: space is scaled down
by $\sqrt{n}$ while time is sped up by $n$. That is exactly the relation $\text{space} \sim 
\sqrt{\text{time}}$ which Definition \ref{def:Brownian-motion} imposes through 
$W_t - W_s \sim \Normal(0,t-s)$, and which reappears as the scaling invariance of Proposition 
\ref{prop:bm-properties}(c). The theorem is the path-space analogue of the Central Limit Theorem, and it explains why
Brownian motion is ubiquitous in the same sense that the Gaussian distribution is.

% Brownian motion is, however, not merely \emph{a} process meeting the requirements
% of Definition \ref{def:Brownian-motion}; it is the process that an enormous class of unrelated
% constructions converges to. Before making that precise we record a general fact about increments
% relative to a filtration, together with the version of Definition \ref{def:Brownian-motion} that it
% suggests, since every statement in the remainder of this chapter is of that relative kind.

\begin{proposition} \label{prop:independent-increments}
Let \(S(t), \, t \geq 0\), be a process adapted to the filtration 
\((\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \geq 0}\) such that for any \(0 \leq s < t\),
the increment \(S(t)-S(s)\) is independent of \(\mathcal{F}_s\). 
Then \(S(t)\) has independent increments.
\end{proposition}

\textbf{\textit{Proof:}} Let $0 \leq t_1 < t_2 < \cdots < t_n$ and write
$\Delta_k = S(t_k) - S(t_{k-1})$. We show by induction on $n$ that the vector
$(\Delta_1,\dots,\Delta_n)$ has independent components. For $n=1$ there is nothing to prove.
Assume the claim for $n-1$. Each of $\Delta_1, \dots, \Delta_{n-1}$ is
$\mathcal{F}_{t_{n-1}}$-measurable, since $S$ is adapted and $t_k \leq t_{n-1}$ for $k \leq n-1$;
hence so is any Borel function of them. By hypothesis $\Delta_n$ is independent of
$\mathcal{F}_{t_{n-1}}$, and therefore independent of $(\Delta_1,\dots,\Delta_{n-1})$. For bounded
Borel $g_1,\dots,g_n$ this gives
\[
\E\Bigl[\prod_{k=1}^n g_k(\Delta_k)\Bigr]
= \E\bigl[g_n(\Delta_n)\bigr]\;\E\Bigl[\prod_{k=1}^{n-1} g_k(\Delta_k)\Bigr]
= \prod_{k=1}^n \E\bigl[g_k(\Delta_k)\bigr],
\]
the last equality by the induction hypothesis. Since this holds for all such $g_k$, the increments
are independent. \hfill $\blacksquare$

\medskip

% Proposition \ref{prop:independent-increments} suggests the notion we shall actually work with. 
% Definition \ref{def:Brownian-motion} describes a process in isolation; every statement in the 
% remainder of this chapter, however, involves a filtration as well: the martingale property, the 
% construction of the Itô integral, Lévy's characterisation and Girsanov's theorem are all assertions 
% about a process \emph{relative to a flow of information}.

\begin{definition}[Brownian motion with respect to a filtration]\label{def:filtbm}
Let $\FPSpace$ be a filtered probability space. A process $W$ is a 
\textbf{$\Filt$-Brownian motion} if
\begin{itemize}
    \item[i)] $W$ is adapted to $\Filt$, with $W_0 = 0$ almost surely and almost all paths continuous; and
    \item[ii)] for all $s \leq t$, the increment $W_t - W_s$ is independent of $\mathcal{F}_s$ and has law $\Normal(0,t-s)$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

By Proposition \ref{prop:independent-increments}, condition ii) already forces the independence of 
increments demanded in Definition \ref{def:Brownian-motion} iv), so a $\Filt$-Brownian motion is in 
particular a Brownian motion; the content of the definition is the \emph{relative} independence, 
which iv) alone does not give. Conversely, any Brownian motion is a $\Filt$-Brownian motion for its 
own natural filtration $\FiltW$, augmented as in Convention \ref{conv:usualconditions}, and this is 
the filtration we use unless another is named.\footnote{
Analogous considerations hold for a Poisson process; see \cite{durrett2019}.} 
The distinction is not idle: Girsanov's theorem (Theorem \ref{girsanovtheorem}) changes the measure 
while keeping the filtration fixed, and its conclusion is exactly that a certain process is a 
$\Filt$-Brownian motion under the new measure.


% One consequence of the Markov property is worth recording in full, both because it is the
% cleanest illustration of what independence of increments buys and because it is the only tool we
% shall have for pricing a contract that looks at the whole path rather than at its endpoint.

% What is needed is the Markov property not at a fixed time but at a \emph{stopping} time.

\begin{theorem}[Strong Markov property]\label{thm:strongmarkov}
Let $W$ be a Brownian motion and $\tau$ an almost surely finite stopping time. Then the shifted
process $\widetilde{W}_s := W_{\tau+s} - W_\tau$, $s \geq 0$, is a Brownian motion independent of
$\mathcal{F}_\tau$ (Definition \ref{def:Ftau}). 
\end{theorem}

\textbf{\textit{Proof:}} See \cite[Thm.\ 2.6.16]{karatzasshreve1991}. \hfill $\blacksquare$

The statement genuinely strengthens Proposition \ref{prop:bm-properties}(d), where the Markov
property was asserted at deterministic times only. Fix $a>0$ and let $\tau_a := \inf\{t \geq 0 : W_t > a\}$ be the first hitting time of the level $a$,
a stopping time by Example \ref{example:hittingtime}. The \emph{reflection principle} is the
observation that, on the event $\{\tau_a \leq t\}$, the post-$\tau_a$ increments form a Brownian
motion independent of $\mathcal{F}_{\tau_a}$, and that a Brownian motion has the same law as its
negative. Reflecting the path in the level $a$ after time $\tau_a$ therefore produces a path of the
same law, and it exchanges the events $\{W_t > a\}$ and $\{W_t < a\}$ while fixing
$\{\tau_a \leq t\}$.

\begin{theorem}[Reflection principle]\label{thm:reflection}
Let $W$ be a Brownian motion. Let $\overline{W}_t := \max_{0\leq s\leq t} W_s$ be its
\textbf{running maximum}. Then for every $a \geq 0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reflection}
\PP\bigl( \overline{W}_t \geq a \bigr) \;=\; \PP\bigl(\tau_a \leq t\bigr) \;=\; 2\,\PP\bigl(W_t > a\bigr) \;=\; \PP\bigl(|W_t| > a\bigr),
\end{equation}
so that $\overline{W}_t$ and $|W_t|$ have the same law. Moreover the pair $(W_t, \overline{W}_t)$ has joint density
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jointmaxdensity}
f_{(W_t,\overline{W}_t)}(x,m) \;=\; \frac{2(2m-x)}{\sqrt{2\pi t^{3}}}\,\exp\left(-\frac{(2m-x)^2}{2t}\right),
\qquad m \geq \max\{x,0\} .
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\textbf{\textit{Proof:}} (Sketch.) The first chain of equalities is the argument just given: $\{\overline{W}_t \geq a\} = \{\tau_a \leq t\}$
because the path is continuous, and conditionally on $\tau_a \leq t$ the reflected path is as likely
to finish above $a$ as below, so $\PP(\tau_a \leq t) = 2\PP(W_t > a)$ --- the boundary case
$W_t = a$ having probability zero. For \eqref{eq:jointmaxdensity} one applies the same reflection to
obtain $\PP(W_t \leq x,\ \overline{W}_t \geq m) = \PP(W_t \geq 2m - x)$ for $m \geq \max\{x,0\}$, and
differentiates in both arguments; see \cite[Sec.\ 2.6]{karatzasshreve1991} or \cite{shreve2004}. \hfill $\blacksquare$

The construction is shown in Figure \ref{fig:reflection}: up to $\tau_a$ the two paths coincide,
and after it the reflected one is the mirror image of the original in the level $a$. Since the
post-$\tau_a$ increments are a Brownian motion independent of $\mathcal{F}_{\tau_a}$, and a Brownian
motion has the same law as its negative, the reflected path is as likely as the original --- which
is exactly what turns the count of paths finishing above $a$ into the count of those that ever
reached it.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Images/reflection.pdf}
\caption[The reflection principle]{The reflection principle. A Brownian path (solid) reaching the
level $a$ for the first time at $\tau_a$, together with the path obtained by reflecting it in that
level after $\tau_a$ (dashed). The two have the same law, they agree on $[0,\tau_a]$, and their
terminal values $W_T$ and $2a-W_T$ lie symmetrically about $a$. Theorem \ref{thm:reflection} is the
statement that this pairing is measure-preserving.}
\label{fig:reflection}
\end{figure}

Two things follow that we shall want later. First, $\E[\overline{W}_t] = \E|W_t| = \sqrt{2t/\pi}$, which
already says that the expected high-water mark of a driftless price over $[0,t]$ grows like
$\sqrt{t}$, on the same scale as the fluctuation itself. Second, \eqref{eq:jointmaxdensity} is a \emph{joint} law across the whole interval,
not a terminal marginal, and it is therefore exactly the object that Breeden--Litzenberger
(Proposition \ref{prop:breedenlitzenberger}) cannot supply. Barrier options, whose payoff depends on
whether $\overline{W}_T$ crossed a level, are priced from \eqref{eq:jointmaxdensity} in the Black--Scholes
model and are precisely the contracts for which the local volatility model of the next chapter
(Subsection \ref{subsec:localvol}), calibrated to terminal marginals alone, gets the answer wrong. We return to
this in Chapter \ref{ch:applications}.

\subsection{Path regularity}\label{subsec:pathregularity}

We now examine how irregular the trajectories of Brownian motion actually are. This subsection is 
short, and every statement in it is classical, but it is the most consequential material in the 
chapter: the construction of the Itô integral in Section \ref{sec:stochasticcalculus} is a 
\emph{response} to what follows, and the theory developed in Part \ref{part:part2} is a response 
to what happens when these estimates are pushed slightly further.

We begin with the observation that the two notions of variation introduced in Subsection
\ref{subsec:martingales}, the quadratic variation of Definition \ref{def:quadratic-variation} and
the total variation of Definition \ref{def:totalvariation}, are in direct competition.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:bmunboundedvariation}
Let $W$ be a Brownian motion. Then, almost surely, $V_1(W)_t = \infty$ for every $t > 0$.
\end{proposition}

\textbf{\textit{Proof:}} Fix $t>0$ and a sequence of partitions $\mathcal{P}_n$ with 
$\lVert\mathcal{P}_n\rVert \to 0$. On any partition,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qvtvbound}
\sum_{k} \bigl(W_{t_k} - W_{t_{k-1}}\bigr)^2 
\;\leq\; \Bigl(\max_{k} \bigl|W_{t_k} - W_{t_{k-1}}\bigr|\Bigr) \cdot \sum_{k}\bigl|W_{t_k}-W_{t_{k-1}}\bigr|
\;\leq\; \Bigl(\max_{k}\bigl|W_{t_k}-W_{t_{k-1}}\bigr|\Bigr)\, V_1(W)_t .
\end{equation}
Suppose, for contradiction, that $V_1(W)_t < \infty$ on an event of positive probability. Sample 
paths of $W$ are continuous, hence uniformly continuous on the compact $[0,t]$, so the maximal 
increment on the right of \eqref{eq:qvtvbound} tends to $0$ as $\lVert\mathcal{P}_n\rVert \to 0$. 
The whole right-hand side therefore tends to $0$, forcing $[W]_t = 0$ on that event. This 
contradicts \eqref{eq:bmqv}, which gives $[W]_t = t > 0$ in $L^2(\Omega)$ and hence almost surely 
along a subsequence. \hfill $\blacksquare$

This result is key because it shows that \textbf{the integral $\int_0^t f\,dW$ cannot be defined pathwise as a
Riemann--Stieltjes integral}, for any non-trivial class of integrands, because $W$ does not induce
a measure on $[0,t]$ along any path. Figure \ref{fig:bmvariation} shows the two sums of
\eqref{eq:qvtvbound} pulling apart as the mesh is refined, which is the whole of Proposition
\ref{prop:bmunboundedvariation} in one picture.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Images/bm-variation.pdf}
\caption[Total variation against quadratic variation]{A Brownian path on $[0,1]$ (left) and its
partition sums (right, logarithmic on both axes) over the dyadic partitions
$\mathcal{P}_n$ with $\lVert\mathcal{P}_n\rVert = 1/n$. The sum of absolute increments grows without
bound, with slope $\tfrac12$ on these axes, so that it diverges like $\sqrt{n}$ --- while the sum of squared
increments settles at $t=1$. This is Proposition \ref{prop:bmunboundedvariation} and
\eqref{eq:bmqv} side by side: the same path has infinite total variation and quadratic variation
exactly $t$, and it is this pair of facts that makes the Riemann--Stieltjes integral unavailable and
the It\^o integral necessary.}
\label{fig:bmvariation}
\end{figure} The same competition can be expressed more finely by asking, not whether the sum of increments 
converges, but for which exponent $p$ the sum of $p$-th powers does.

% Every difficulty in Section \ref{sec:stochasticcalculus} --- 
% the restriction to adapted integrands, the insistence on evaluating at the left endpoint, the 
% approximation in $L^2(\Omega)$ rather than pathwise, the appearance of a second-order term in the 
% chain rule --- traces back to \eqref{eq:qvtvbound}. Itô's construction is not one possible approach 
% among several; it is a way of exploiting probabilistic structure to recover an object which 
% deterministic analysis cannot reach.



\begin{definition}[$p$-variation]\label{def:pvariation}
Let $p \geq 1$. The \textbf{$p$-variation} of $f : [0,T] \to \R$ on $[0,t]$ is
\begin{equation}
V_p(f)_t := \left( \sup_{\mathcal{P}} \sum_{k=1}^n \bigl|f(t_k)-f(t_{k-1})\bigr|^p \right)^{1/p},
\end{equation}
and $f$ is said to have \emph{finite $p$-variation} on $[0,t]$ if $V_p(f)_t < \infty$. We write 
$f \in \mathcal{C}^{p\text{-var}}([0,t])$.
\end{definition}

For $p = 1$ this is Definition \ref{def:totalvariation}. For $p=2$ it is \emph{not} the
quadratic variation of Definition \ref{def:quadratic-variation}, and the difference is not cosmetic:
the latter is a limit along partitions of vanishing mesh, the former a supremum over all partitions,
and for Brownian motion the first equals $t$ while the second is infinite (Theorem
\ref{thm:bmregularity} ii)). Refining a partition can only increase the sum of squared increments,
so the supremum sees the worst partition rather than the fine ones. If 
$f$ is continuous and $V_p(f)_t < \infty$, then $V_q(f)_t < \infty$ for every $q > p$, since 
$|f(t_k)-f(t_{k-1})|^q \leq (\max_k |f(t_k)-f(t_{k-1})|)^{q-p} |f(t_k)-f(t_{k-1})|^p$. Each 
function thus has a critical exponent below which its $p$-variation is infinite and above which it 
is finite, and this exponent is a precise measure of roughness.

\begin{theorem}[Regularity of Brownian paths]\label{thm:bmregularity}
Let $W$ be a Brownian motion. Then, almost surely:
\begin{itemize}
    \item[i)] the sample path $t \mapsto W_t$ is locally $\gamma$-Hölder continuous for every 
    $\gamma < \tfrac12$, and is locally $\gamma$-Hölder for no $\gamma \geq \tfrac12$;
    \item[ii)] $V_p(W)_t < \infty$ for every $p > 2$ and every $t$, whereas $V_p(W)_t = \infty$ for 
    every $p \leq 2$;
    \item[iii)] the sample path is nowhere differentiable: for almost every $\omega$, there is no 
    $t$ at which $t \mapsto W_t(\omega)$ possesses a finite derivative. \hfill $\square$
\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

Part i) was half-proved above via Kolmogorov's criterion; the negative half, like iii), is due to 
Paley, Wiener and Zygmund, and proofs of all three may be found in \cite{falconer} or 
\cite{karatzasshreve1991}. Part iii) is worth a moment's reflection. A continuous function 
which is differentiable nowhere is already a celebrated pathology in real analysis, exhibited with 
some effort by Weierstrass; the theorem says that if one picks a Brownian path at random one 
obtains such a function with probability one. The pathological case is the meaningful case for integration.



\begin{remark}[The critical exponent]\label{rem:criticalexponent}
Brownian motion has finite $p$-variation exactly for $p > 2$. The number $2$ is a threshold in a 
strong sense, and the reason is the following. Young's integral $\int g\,df$ can be constructed
\emph{pathwise} \cite{young1936}, by purely deterministic means and with no probability involved,
whenever $f$ has finite $p$-variation, $g$ has finite $q$-variation, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:youngcondition}
\tfrac1p + \tfrac1q > 1 .
\end{equation}
The threshold $2$ is not part of \eqref{eq:youngcondition} but a consequence of it in the case that 
matters to us. The integrands we shall meet are of the form $g = F(f)$ for smooth $F$, which 
inherits the regularity of $f$ itself, so $q = p$ and \eqref{eq:youngcondition} collapses to 
$2/p > 1$, that is $p < 2$. Brownian motion misses this regime by an amount 
that no refinement of the estimate can recover, since $V_2(W)_t = \infty$.

The Itô integral of Section \ref{sec:stochasticcalculus} is the tool that bridges this 
gap, and it does so by spending probabilistic structure: the integrand must be adapted, the 
approximation is in $L^2(\Omega)$ rather than pathwise, and the resulting object is defined only 
up to null sets. It buys us the entire theory of Part \ref{part:part1}, but it also means 
the theory is tied to a specific process and a specific filtration, and it is exactly this 
dependence which becomes an obstacle when the driving signal is rougher than Brownian --- when 
even $p = 2$ is out of reach, and neither Young nor Itô applies. The theory that operates in that 
regime is the subject of Part \ref{part:part2}.
\end{remark}

\section{Stochastic Calculus}\label{sec:stochasticcalculus}

Stochastic calculus is a broad area of mathematics which allows, among other things, randomness and uncertainty to be incorporated into differential equations. One of our main aims is to give meaning to expressions of the kind 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sde_intro}
dX_t = a(t,X_t)\,dt + b(t,X_t)\,dW_t, 
\quad X_0 \in \R.
\end{equation}
Here $dW_t$ will be specified in the next subsection. Notice that this generalises the deterministic case: if $b \equiv 0$, then $X_t$ reduces to the solution of the ODE $dX_t = a(t,X_t)\,dt$. What is being modelled is still a trajectory --- a \textit{position} as a function of time --- but a random one, drawn from a whole family of possible paths; the evolution of the underlying probability distribution then follows as a consequence, and we shall derive it explicitly in Subsection \ref{section:itoDiff}. It is this second, distributional description that most applications end up using, and pricing is no exception.

We shall first construct a rigorous integral interpretation of such expressions, providing concrete meaning to the stochastic term. Afterwards, we will introduce the stochastic chain rule (Itô's Lemma), which differs from the classical one in a somewhat surprising yet elegant way, and then proceed to study the existence and uniqueness of solutions and some of their properties.

The exposition below could be carried out in the full generality anticipated by the semimartingales of Definition \ref{def:semimartingale}. We keep instead to the classical presentation, integrating against Brownian motion alone, and record at the end of the section what the general case would require. The aim is to reach the results we actually need without losing sight of them along the way.

\subsection{The Itô Integral}\label{subsec:itointegral}

The stochastic differential equation \eqref{eq:sde_intro} can be reformulated in integral form as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sde_integral}
X_t - X_0 = \int_0^t a(s,X_s)\,ds \;+\; \int_0^t b(s,X_s)\,dW_s.
\end{equation}
For each $\omega \in \Omega$, the first integral on the right-hand side is nothing new. Regarding the second one, we may ask whether there is a sensible way of defining
\[
M_t = \int_0^t f(s,\omega)\,dW_s, \quad 0 \leq t \leq T,
\]
so that $\{M_t\}_{t\in[0,T]}$ is a martingale. Another important consideration is that the integrand must be \emph{adapted} to the filtration associated with $W_t$. These requirements naturally lead to the following definitions.

\begin{definition}[Adapted square-integrable processes]\label{def:L2_adapted}
Let $\FPSpace$ be a filtered probability space.
\begin{itemize}
    \item[i)] The space of stochastic processes on $[0,T]$, adapted to a filtration $(\mathcal{F}_t)_{t\geq 0}$ and such that 
    \[
    \int_0^T \E[|f(t)|^2]\,dt < \infty,
    \]
    will be denoted by $L^2_{\mathrm{ad}}([0,T]\times \Omega)$. This is a Hilbert space with the inner product
    \[
    \langle f,g \rangle = \int_0^T \E[f(t)g(t)]\,dt.
    \]

    \item[ii)] We say $f \in L^2_{\mathrm{ad}}([0,T]\times \Omega)$ is a \textbf{step stochastic process} if
    \[
    f(t,\omega) = \sum_{i=1}^n \psi(t_{i-1},\omega)\,\mathds{1}_{[t_{i-1},t_i)}(t),
    \]
    where $\psi(t_{i-1})$ is $\mathcal{F}_{t_{i-1}}$-measurable and has finite variance ($\E[\psi(t_{i-1})^2] < \infty$).
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\medskip

The construction of the stochastic integral begins by defining it on step processes and extending by density. For such $f$, take $t_0=0$, $t_n=T$, and define\footnote{The choice of the left endpoint $t_{i-1}$ ensures that the stochastic integral preserves the martingale property. Variants with different evaluation points lead to different calculi, e.g. Stratonovich calculus, where the classical chain rule holds (see \cite{kuo2006}).}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:integral_step}
I(f) = \int_0^T f(t)\,dW_t := \sum_{i=1}^n \psi(t_{i-1})\,\big(W(t_i)-W(t_{i-1})\big).
\end{equation}
This mapping $f \mapsto I(f)$ is linear. The following lemmas are key.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:isometry_step}
If $f$ is a step stochastic process, then
\[
\E[I(f)] = 0, 
\quad 
\E[I(f)^2] = \int_0^T \E[f(t)^2]\,dt.
\]
\end{lemma}
\textbf{\textit{Proof:}} Write $\Delta_i W = W(t_i)-W(t_{i-1})$, so that
$I(f) = \sum_{i=1}^n \psi(t_{i-1})\Delta_i W$. Since $\psi(t_{i-1})$ is
$\mathcal{F}_{t_{i-1}}$-measurable and $\Delta_i W$ is independent of $\mathcal{F}_{t_{i-1}}$ with
mean zero, conditioning gives
\[
\E\bigl[\psi(t_{i-1})\Delta_i W\bigr]
= \E\Bigl[\psi(t_{i-1})\,\E\bigl[\Delta_i W \mid \mathcal{F}_{t_{i-1}}\bigr]\Bigr] = 0,
\]
and summing yields $\E[I(f)]=0$. For the second moment, expand the square. If $i<j$ then
$\psi(t_{i-1})\psi(t_{j-1})\Delta_i W$ is $\mathcal{F}_{t_{j-1}}$-measurable while $\Delta_j W$
is independent of it with mean zero, so the cross terms vanish by the same argument. The diagonal
terms give, using independence of $\Delta_i W$ from $\mathcal{F}_{t_{i-1}}$ once more,
\[
\E\bigl[\psi(t_{i-1})^2 (\Delta_i W)^2\bigr]
= \E\bigl[\psi(t_{i-1})^2\bigr](t_i - t_{i-1}) = \int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_i}\E[f(t)^2]\,dt,
\]
since $f \equiv \psi(t_{i-1})$ on $[t_{i-1},t_i)$. Summing over $i$ gives
$\E[I(f)^2] = \int_0^T \E[f(t)^2]\,dt$. \hfill $\blacksquare$

\medskip

\noindent Note that both computations rest on exactly one structural fact: the integrand is evaluated
at the \emph{left} endpoint, which is what makes $\psi(t_{i-1})$ independent of the increment it
multiplies. Evaluating anywhere else destroys both identities.

\begin{lemma}[Density of step processes]\label{lem:density}
For every $f \in L^2_{\mathrm{ad}}([0,T]\times \Omega)$, there exists a sequence $\{f_n\}_{n=1}^\infty$ of step stochastic processes such that
\[
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^T \E[(f(t)-f_n(t))^2]\,dt = 0.
\]
That is, step processes are dense in $L^2_{\mathrm{ad}}([0,T]\times \Omega)$.
\end{lemma}
\textbf{\textit{Proof:}} The argument is given in Appendix \ref{app:proof:density}; see also \cite[Lemma 4.3.3]{kuo2006}. \hfill $\blacksquare$

\medskip

We are now ready to extend the definition.

\begin{definition}[Stochastic Integral]\label{def:stochastic_integral}
Let $f \in L^2_{\mathrm{ad}}([0,T]\times\Omega)$. We define its \emph{stochastic integral} over $[0,T]$ as the limit in $L^2(\Omega)$ of $I(f_n)$ for any approximating sequence of step processes $\{f_n\}$ with $f_n \to f$ in $L^2_{\mathrm{ad}}([0,T]\times\Omega)$.
\end{definition}

The following fundamental result follows.

\begin{theorem}[Itô's isometry]\label{thm:itoisometry}

Let $f \in L^2_{\mathrm{ad}}([0,T]\times \Omega)$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item[i)] The stochastic integral $I(f)$ is a centred random variable and
    \[
    \E[I(f)^2] = \int_0^T \E[f(t)^2]\,dt.
    \]

    \item[ii)] For another $g \in L^2_{\mathrm{ad}}([0,T]\times\Omega)$,
    \[
    \E\left[\left(\int_0^T f(t)\,dW_t\right)\left(\int_0^T g(t)\,dW_t\right)\right]
    = \int_0^T \E[f(t)g(t)]\,dt.
    \]
\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}
\textbf{\textit{Proof:}} i) Let $\{f_n\}$ be step processes with $f_n \to f$ in
$L^2_{\mathrm{ad}}([0,T]\times\Omega)$, as furnished by Lemma \ref{lem:density}. By Lemma
\ref{lem:isometry_step} applied to $f_n - f_m$,
\[
\E\bigl[(I(f_n)-I(f_m))^2\bigr] = \int_0^T \E\bigl[(f_n(t)-f_m(t))^2\bigr]\,dt
\xrightarrow[n,m\to\infty]{} 0,
\]
so $\{I(f_n)\}$ is Cauchy in $L^2(\Omega)$ and its limit $I(f)$ exists and is independent of the
approximating sequence. Both $f \mapsto \E[I(f)]$ and $f \mapsto \E[I(f)^2]$ are
continuous for the $L^2$ norms involved, so the identities $\E[I(f_n)]=0$ and
$\E[I(f_n)^2] = \int_0^T\E[f_n^2]\,dt$ pass to the limit.

ii) Apply i) to $f+g$ and expand:
$\E[I(f+g)^2] = \E[I(f)^2] + 2\E[I(f)I(g)] + \E[I(g)^2]$ by linearity
of $I$, while the right-hand side expands identically. Cancelling the square terms gives the stated
polarisation. \hfill $\blacksquare$

\medskip

\noindent In the language of Proposition \ref{prop:condjensenl2}, item ii), this is precisely
the statement that $I$ is an isometry from $L^2_{\mathrm{ad}}([0,T]\times\Omega)$ into $L^2(\Omega)$, extended
from a dense subspace by continuity --- the standard extension of a bounded operator, and the reason
the construction works at all.

It is worth noting that if $f$ is deterministic (i.e. does not depend on $\omega$), then
\[
I(f) \sim \Normal\!\left(0, \int_0^T f(t)^2\,dt\right).
\]

For $0\leq t \leq T$, we have $\int_0^t \E[f(s)^2]\,ds \leq \int_0^T \E[f(s)^2]\,ds$, so $f\in L^2_{\mathrm{ad}}([0,t]\times \Omega)$ and the stochastic integral is well defined on $[0,t]$. Consider then the process $X_t = \int_0^t f(s) dW(s) .$ We know this process has finite first two moments, and so we can take the conditional expectation of $X_t$ with respect to the given filtration. 

\begin{theorem}[Martingale property]\label{theorem:martingale_property}

Take $f\in L^2_{\mathrm{ad}}([0,T]\times\Omega). $ Then the process given by $X_t = I(f \mathds{1}_{[0,t]}) $ is a martingale with respect to the   filtration $\mathcal{F}_t.$
    
\end{theorem}

\textbf{\textit{Proof:}} Integrability follows from Itô's isometry, since
$\E[X_t^2] = \int_0^t \E[f(s)^2]\,ds < \infty$ and, for probability spaces, $L^2(\Omega) \subset L^1(\Omega)$. Adaptedness follows from the approximating step processes.

For the martingale identity, take first $f$ a step process and $s<t$ lying in the partition defining
it (refining the partition if necessary, which changes nothing). Then
$X_t - X_s = \sum_{i} \psi(t_{i-1})\bigl(W(t_i)-W(t_{i-1})\bigr)$, the sum running over the
subintervals of $[s,t]$. For each such $i$ we have $t_{i-1} \geq s$, so conditioning first on
$\mathcal{F}_{t_{i-1}}$ and then on $\mathcal{F}_s$ gives
\[
\E\bigl[\psi(t_{i-1})(W(t_i)-W(t_{i-1})) \mid \mathcal{F}_s\bigr]
= \E\Bigl[\psi(t_{i-1})\,\E\bigl[W(t_i)-W(t_{i-1}) \mid \mathcal{F}_{t_{i-1}}\bigr] \,\Big|\, \mathcal{F}_s\Bigr] = 0,
\]
by the tower property (Proposition \ref{prop:condexpproperties}, item 5) and independence of the
increment from $\mathcal{F}_{t_{i-1}}$. Hence $\E[X_t \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = X_s$.

For general $f \in L^2_{\mathrm{ad}}$, take step processes $f_n \to f$ and write $X^n$ for the corresponding
integrals. Itô's isometry gives $X^n_t \to X_t$ in $L^2(\Omega)$, hence in $L^1(\Omega)$, and
conditional expectation is a contraction on $L^1$ (Proposition \ref{prop:condexpproperties},
item 7), so $\E[X^n_t \mid \mathcal{F}_s] \to \E[X_t \mid \mathcal{F}_s]$ in
$L^1(\Omega)$. The left-hand side equals $X^n_s \to X_s$, and the martingale identity passes to the
limit. \hfill $\blacksquare$

The Markov property also holds, but we will return to that when we see these processes as diffusions in Subsection \ref{section:itoDiff}.  We shall now address the continuity of the process $X_t$. We must keep in mind that this integral is not defined for each $\omega$ in a Riemann sense. The following result is therefore not as elementary as in real analysis.

\begin{theorem}[Continuity property]\label{thm:itocontinuity}
    Suppose $f\in L^2_{\mathrm{ad}}([0,T]\times\Omega). $ Then the process $X_t = \int_0^t f(s)dW(s)$ admits a continuous modification, with which we shall always work.
\end{theorem}
\textbf{\textit{Proof:}} The argument is given in Appendix \ref{app:proof:itocontinuity}; see also \cite[Thm.\ 4.6.2]{kuo2006}. \hfill $\blacksquare$

\medskip

\noindent This is the payoff of Proposition \ref{prop:dooblp} announced earlier: the maximal
inequality is exactly what upgrades convergence at each fixed time to uniform convergence along a
subsequence, and hence $L^2$ convergence to a pathwise statement.

Finally, a result that actually links Riemann sums with stochastic integrals will be helpful in the next subsection.

\begin{theorem}\label{theorem:riemannian_limit}
    Suppose $f\in L^2_{\mathrm{ad}}([0,T]\times\Omega)$ and assume that $\E[f(t)f(s)]$ is a continuous function of $t$ and $s$. Then, for  partitions of $[0,T] $, given by $\mathcal{P}=\{0=t_0<t_1<\cdots<t_n=T\}$,

\begin{equation}
\int_0^T f(t)\, dW(t)
= \lim_{\|\mathcal P\|\to 0} \sum_{i=1}^n f(t_{i-1})\bigl(W(t_i)-W(t_{i-1})\bigr)
\end{equation}
in $L^2(\Omega),$ where the limit is taken as in Definition \ref{def:quadratic-variation}.
\end{theorem}
\textbf{\textit{Proof:}} The argument is given in Appendix \ref{app:proof:riemannlimit}; see also \cite[Thm.\ 4.7.1]{kuo2006}. \hfill $\blacksquare$

\medskip

\noindent The continuity hypothesis on $\E[f(t)f(s)]$ is what makes this work and cannot
simply be dropped: without it the left-endpoint approximation need not converge, and the Riemann-sum
interpretation of the Itô integral fails.

Theorem \ref{theorem:riemannian_limit} also isolates the one genuinely arbitrary choice in the whole
construction, and it is worth naming the alternative, because Chapter \ref{ch:roughpaths} will show that
the two are on an equal footing.

\begin{definition}[Stratonovich integral]\label{def:stratonovich}
The \textbf{Stratonovich integral} is defined, under the hypotheses of Theorem
\ref{theorem:riemannian_limit}, as the limit of the same Riemann sums with the integrand
evaluated at the \emph{midpoint} of each subinterval rather than at the left endpoint:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stratonovich}
\int_0^T f(t)\circ dW(t) := \lim_{\lVert\mathcal{P}\rVert\to0}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
f\Bigl(\tfrac{t_{i-1}+t_i}{2}\Bigr)\bigl(W(t_i)-W(t_{i-1})\bigr),
\end{equation}
the limit again being taken in $L^2(\Omega)$.
\end{definition}

For integrands of the form $f = F(W)$ with $F$ smooth the two integrals both exist and differ by a
computable amount,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:itostratonovich}
\int_0^T F(W_t)\circ dW_t = \int_0^T F(W_t)\,dW_t + \tfrac12\int_0^T F'(W_t)\,dt ,
\end{equation}
the correction being one half of the quadratic covariation $\langle F(W),W\rangle_T$. The Itô
integral is the one we shall use throughout Part \ref{part:part1}, for the reason given in the
footnote above: it alone preserves the martingale property, because it evaluates the integrand
before the increment it multiplies. The Stratonovich integral buys something else in exchange, namely
the classical chain rule with no second-order term, which makes it the natural choice in differential
geometry and in the Wong--Zakai approximation results of Chapter \ref{ch:roughpaths}. Neither is more
correct than the other; they answer different questions, and \eqref{eq:itostratonovich} is the
dictionary. Chapter \ref{ch:roughpaths} will recover this dictionary as a special case of a general
formula, and will identify the choice between the two as a choice of \emph{lift} rather than a
choice of convention.

More generally, we can consider a larger family of integrands. Let $\mathcal{L}(\Omega,L^2[0,T])$ be the family of processes $f$ such that
\begin{itemize}
    \item $f(t)$ is $\mathcal{F}_t-$adapted.
    \item $\int_0^T |f(t)| ^2 \, dt < \infty$ almost surely.
\end{itemize}
The class $\mathcal{L}(\Omega,L^1[0,T])$, which we shall also need, is defined in exactly the same
way with $\int_0^T |f(t)|\,dt < \infty$ almost surely in place of the second condition.

Notice how we dropped the condition of having finite expectation, so that we have indeed a larger class of processes. Consider for example the process $f(t) =e^{W(t)^2}.$ It follows that 

\begin{empheq}[left={\E[f(t)^2]= \empheqlbrace}]{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
      &\frac 1 {\sqrt{1-4t}} & & \text{if } 0\leq t< 1/4\\[1ex]
      & \infty & & \text{if } t \geq 1/4.
  \end{aligned}
\end{empheq}

Hence $\int_0^1 e^{W(t)^2}\, dW_t$ does not make sense with the previous construction. These processes are nonetheless worth retaining, and the following holds; its proof lies beyond our scope.

\begin{theorem}[Local Martingale Property] Let $f\in \mathcal{L}(\Omega,L^2[0,T])$ and $t \in [0,T]$. Then 
$$
X_ t = \int_0^t f(s) \,dW_s
$$
    is a local martingale. Furthermore, it has a continuous realisation. \hfill $\square$
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Itô's Chain Rule}

One of the most striking features of this new integration is a modified chain rule, generalising the familiar expression $d (f(g))(x) = f'(g(x))dg(x)$. The key underlying fact for this new integration rule is the non-zero quadratic variation of Brownian motion. This new calculus allows us to find some new relations and solve new equations that naturally generalise the already rich world of differential equations.

\begin{definition}[Itô process]

Given an initial random variable $X_0$, an Itô process is a stochastic process on $[0,T]$ of the form 

$$
X_t = X_0 + \int_0^t g(s) \,ds  +\int_0^t f(s) \, dW_s  \iff dX_t = g(t) \,dt + f(t) \, dW_t, \text{ } \text{ } X(0) = X_0
$$
    where we emphasise the equivalent formulations, since the latter is just a way of writing the former. We impose $X_0$ being $\mathcal F_0$-measurable, $g \in \mathcal{L}(\Omega,L^1[0,T])$ and $f \in \mathcal{L}(\Omega,L^2[0,T]).$ Note how the first integral is just a Lebesgue integral for each $\omega$, that is, for each path of $W$. The second is a proper Itô integral.
\end{definition}

We directly state the more general form of Itô's new calculus rule, in the convenient \textit{differential formulation}. A complete proof is beyond the scope of this Thesis. However, such a renowned Theorem has been addressed by several authors, for example in \cite{kuo2006}.

\begin{theorem}[Itô's Rule]\label{theorem:itorule}
    Let $dX_t = \mu(t)\,dt + \sigma(t)\,dW_t$ be an Itô process, and let 
    $V(t,x)$ be a continuous function on $[0,T] \times \R$ with continuous partial derivatives 
    $\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}$, $\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}$ and $\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2}$.
    Then $V(t,X_t)$ is also an Itô process, and
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:ito_formula}
        dV(t,X_t) 
        = \left(
            \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}(t,X_t)
            + \mu(t)\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}(t,X_t)
            + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2(t)\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2}(t,X_t)
        \right) dt
        + \sigma(t)\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}(t,X_t)\,dW_t.
    \end{equation}
\qedopen
\end{theorem}

\noindent
A convenient way to derive Equation~\eqref{eq:ito_formula} is through a symbolic
derivation using the Taylor expansion to first order in $dt$ and second order in $dX_t$,
together with the \textit{Itô table} of Table \ref{tab:ItoTableScalar}.

\begin{center}
\[
\begin{array}{c|cc}
\times & dW_t & dt \\ \hline
dW_t & dt & 0 \\
dt & 0 & 0
\end{array}
\]
{\captionof{table}{Itô multiplication table for a scalar Brownian motion.}%
\label{tab:ItoTableScalar}}
\end{center}

\noindent
Applying the Taylor expansion to $V(t,X_t)$ gives
\[
dV(t,X_t)
= \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}(t,X_t)\,dt
+ \frac{\partial V}{\partial x}(t,X_t)\,dX_t
+ \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2}(t,X_t)\,(dX_t)^2.
\]
Using the Itô table, we have $(dX_t)^2 = \sigma(t)^2\,dt$, so using
$dX_t = \mu(t)\,dt + \sigma(t)\,dW_t$ yields
\[
dV(t,X_t)
= \frac{\partial V}{\partial x}(t,X_t)\sigma(t)\,dW_t
+ \left[
\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}(t,X_t)
+ \mu(t)\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}(t,X_t)
+ \frac{1}{2}\sigma(t)^2\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2}(t,X_t)
\right] dt,
\]
which is precisely Equation~\eqref{eq:ito_formula}.

We stress that the computation just performed is a \emph{mnemonic}, not a proof: the symbols $dW_t$ and $(dW_t)^2$ have been manipulated as though they were infinitesimals, which they are not. What makes the manipulation legitimate is the content of \eqref{eq:bmqv} --- the second-order term survives precisely because $W$ has non-vanishing quadratic variation --- and a genuine proof proceeds by Taylor-expanding along a partition and controlling the remainder in $L^2(\Omega)$. We refer to \cite{kuo2006} for the details. The value of the Itô table is that it makes the bookkeeping automatic once one accepts that $\langle W \rangle_t = t$, and we shall use it in that spirit throughout.

As with the ordinary chain rule, this opens the way to evaluating stochastic integrals in terms of others.

\begin{example}
 We can use this result to obtain a closed form for the integral $\int_0^t W_s\,dW_s$. Take $g(x) = x^2$ and define $Y_t = g(W_t)$, so that
 \begin{equation}
     dY_t = 2W_t\,dW_t + \frac 2 2 \,(dW_t)^2 = dt + 2 W_t\,dW_t
 \end{equation}
 so that $\int_0^t W_s\,dW_s = \frac 1 2 \left( W_t^2 - t\right)$, which is a much simpler expression, and clearly a martingale.
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{example:blackscholessde}
    In Finance, the Black--Scholes model relies on the \textit{log-normal} equation, given by $dS_t = rS_t \, dt + \sigma S_t \, dW_t$, where $r, \sigma \in \R$ and $\sigma > 0$. It can be solved by taking $Y_t = \ln (S_t),$ so that
    \begin{equation}
        dY_t = \frac 1 S_t dS_t -\frac{1}{2S_t^2}(dS_t)^2 = r\,dt + \sigma\, dW_t -  \frac 1 2 \sigma^2 \, dt
    \end{equation}

    obtaining $Y_t = Y_0 + (r-\sigma^2 / 2) \, t + \sigma W_t \implies S_t = S_0e^{(r-\sigma^2/ 2)\, t + \sigma \, W_t} $.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Lamperti's Transform]

     Consider a process  $dX_t = \mu(t,X_t)\, dt + \sigma(t,X_t) \, dW_t$ and assume that $\sigma$ is \emph{strictly positive} on the range of $X$ --- without which the integrand below is not defined --- and differentiable once with respect to time and once with respect to $x$, which is what is needed for $\Gamma \in \mathcal{C}^{1,2}$ and hence for Itô's rule to apply. Fix any reference point $x_0$ in the range of $X$ and define the \emph{deterministic} function 
    \begin{equation}
        \Gamma(t,x) = \int_{x_0}^x \frac{1}{\sigma(t,s)}\, ds
    \end{equation}
The reference point must be a constant and not, say, $X_0$: Itô's rule requires a deterministic
$\mathcal{C}^{1,2}$ function, and changing $x_0$ shifts $\Gamma$ by a function of $t$ alone, which
alters the drift below but not the conclusion. Consider now the process $Y_t = \Gamma(t,X_t).$ After a few calculations, we arrive to
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        dY_t &= \left( \frac{\partial \Gamma}{\partial t} + \mu(t,X_t)\frac{\partial \Gamma}{\partial x} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2(t,X_t)\frac{\partial^2 \Gamma}{\partial x^2} \right) dt + \underbrace{\sigma(t,X_t)\frac{\partial \Gamma}{\partial x}}_{=1} \, dW_t \\
        &= \left( \frac{\partial \Gamma}{\partial t} + \frac{\mu(t,X_t)}{\sigma(t,X_t)} - \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial \sigma}{\partial x}(t,X_t) \right) dt + dW_t
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
so that all the complexity of the original process is contained in the drift. What the transform
buys is worth stating precisely: the diffusion coefficient of $Y$ is identically $1$, so that $Y$ is
driven by the Brownian increments themselves and an increment of $Y$ over a step of length
$\Delta t$ has variance $\Delta t$ whatever the current state. Two consequences follow, and they are
the reason the transform is used in practice. A lattice built on $Y$ moves by the same amount at
every node, which is exactly the condition under which a binomial or trinomial tree
\emph{recombines}, so that the number of nodes grows quadratically rather than exponentially in the
number of steps. And a discretisation of $Y$ no longer has to approximate a varying diffusion
coefficient, which is the term limiting the accuracy of the Euler scheme applied to $X$ directly.
\end{example}

\subsection{Stochastic Differential Equations}\label{subsec:sdes}

The main aim of this subsection is to give conditions under which an Itô process exists and is unique. We follow a direct approach and state the existence and uniqueness theorem. Note that we work throughout with the differential form of the equations, while keeping in mind that its meaning is the integral one given above. We need a precise definition for \textit{stochastic differential equation} or SDE, and thus some preliminary results.

Given an $\mathcal{F}_0$-measurable random variable $X_0$ of finite variance, consider the following formal equation on $[0,T]$: 

\begin{equation}\label{sde_template}
    dX_t = \mu(t,X_t) \, dt + \sigma ( t, X_t) \, dW_t \text{ }\text{ }\text{ }\text{ }\text{ }\text{ } X(0)= X_0.
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}[Solution of an SDE]\label{def:sdesolution}
    A stochastic process $X_t$, $0\leq t \leq T$, is called a solution of \eqref{sde_template} if it satisfies:

    \begin{itemize}
        \item $\sigma(t,X_t) \in \mathcal{L}(\Omega,L^2[0,T])$ so its Itô integral makes sense for each $t\in [0,T].$
        \item Almost all sample paths of $\mu(t,X_t)$ belong to $L^1[0,T]$.
        \item Equation \eqref{sde_template} holds almost surely.
     \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

We need to establish some growth conditions on $\mu$ and $\sigma$ for the equation to make sense on, at least, an open interval, just like in ordinary differential equations. Let us define the following conditions:

\begin{definition}\label{def:lipschitzgrowth}
    Let $g(t,x)$  a measurable function on $[0,T] \times \R$.

    \begin{itemize}
        \item It is said to satisfy a Lipschitz condition (or simply called Lipschitz) in $x$ if there exists $K>0$ such that $|g(t,x)-g(t,y) |\leq K |x-y| $ for all $0 \leq t \leq T,$ $x,y\in\R.$
        \item Similarly, it is said to satisfy a linear growth condition in x if there exists $K>0$ such that $|g(t,x)|\leq K(1+|x|)$ for all $0 \leq t \leq T,$ $x\in\R.$
    \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

Now, notice that for $x\geq 0$, it holds that $1+x^2 \leq (1+x) ^2 \leq 2(1+x^2)$  so that the linear growth condition can be restated in terms of a new constant $M$ such that $|g(t,x)| \leq M \sqrt{1+x^2}.$ 

The proof of existence and uniqueness rests on a Picard iteration, exactly as for ordinary differential equations, and the comparison step is furnished by Grönwall's inequality (Lemma \ref{lem:gronwall} in Appendix \ref{app:auxiliary}); since the argument is deterministic and entirely standard we do not reproduce it here.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:sdeexistence}
    Let $\mu$ and $\sigma$ be measurable functions on $[0,T] \times \R$ satisfying both the Lipschitz and the linear growth conditions in $x$ of Definition \ref{def:lipschitzgrowth}, and let the initial condition $X_0$ be $\mathcal{F}_0$-measurable with $\E[X_0^2] < \infty$. Then the stochastic differential equation \eqref{sde_template} admits a solution, which is unique in the following sense: any two solutions are indistinguishable. Moreover the solution has continuous sample paths.
\end{theorem}
\textbf{\textit{Proof:}}
We shall only prove the uniqueness part, the existence being a Picard iteration as indicated above.
Let $X$ and $Y$ be two continuous solutions and put $Z_t = X_t - Y_t$, so that
\[
Z_t = \int_0^t \bigl[\sigma(s,X_s)-\sigma(s,Y_s)\bigr]dW_s + \int_0^t \bigl[\mu(s,X_s)-\mu(s,Y_s)\bigr]ds .
\]
We cannot yet take expectations in this identity. By Definition \ref{def:sdesolution} the integrand
of the stochastic term lies only in the pathwise class $\mathcal{L}(\Omega,L^2[0,T])$, on which the
Itô integral is constructed by localisation and is a local martingale rather than a martingale; Itô's
isometry, a statement about $L^2_{\mathrm{ad}}([0,T]\times\Omega)$, is therefore not available. Nor do we know
that $\E[Z_t^2]$ is finite, and an inequality between infinite quantities would tell us
nothing. Both obstructions are removed by the same device. Define, for $n \in \N$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sdelocalising}
\tau_n := \inf\bigl\{t \in [0,T] : |X_t| \geq n \ \text{ or } \ |Y_t| \geq n\bigr\} \wedge T ,
\end{equation}
which is a stopping time by Proposition \ref{prop:stoppingtimechar}, since $X$ and $Y$ are continuous
and adapted. As their paths are continuous, they are bounded on $[0,T]$ for almost every $\omega$,
whence $\tau_n \uparrow T$ almost surely.

Fix $n$ and stop the identity above at $\tau_n$. On $\{s \leq \tau_n\}$ the Lipschitz condition gives
$|\sigma(s,X_s)-\sigma(s,Y_s)| \leq K|Z_s| \leq 2Kn$, so the stopped integrand
$\mathds{1}_{[0,\tau_n]}(s)\bigl[\sigma(s,X_s)-\sigma(s,Y_s)\bigr]$ is bounded, adapted, and hence
genuinely an element of $L^2_{\mathrm{ad}}([0,T]\times\Omega)$. Itô's isometry now applies to it, and together
with the Lipschitz condition yields
\[
\begin{aligned}
\E\left[\left(\int_0^{t\wedge\tau_n} \bigl[\sigma(s,X_s)-\sigma(s,Y_s)\bigr]dW_s\right)^{\!2}\right]
&= \int_0^{t} \E\bigl[(\sigma(s,X_s)-\sigma(s,Y_s))^2\,\mathds{1}_{\{s\leq\tau_n\}}\bigr]ds \\[2pt]
&\leq K^2\!\int_0^t \E\bigl[Z_s^2\,\mathds{1}_{\{s\leq\tau_n\}}\bigr]ds .
\end{aligned}
\]
For the Lebesgue term, the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality followed by the Lipschitz condition gives,
pathwise,
\[
\left(\int_0^{t\wedge\tau_n} \bigl[\mu(s,X_s)-\mu(s,Y_s)\bigr]ds\right)^{\!2}
\leq T\int_0^{t} \bigl(\mu(s,X_s)-\mu(s,Y_s)\bigr)^2 \mathds{1}_{\{s\leq\tau_n\}}\,ds
\leq T K^2\!\int_0^t Z_s^2\,\mathds{1}_{\{s\leq\tau_n\}}\,ds .
\]
Write $u_n(t) := \E\bigl[Z_{t\wedge\tau_n}^2\bigr]$. Since $|Z_{t\wedge\tau_n}| \leq 2n$ we
have $u_n(t) \leq 4n^2 < \infty$ for every $t$, which is the finiteness the argument was missing.
Moreover $Z_s^2\,\mathds{1}_{\{s\leq\tau_n\}} \leq Z_{s\wedge\tau_n}^2$ pointwise, so combining the
two estimates through $(a+b)^2 \leq 2(a^2+b^2)$ and setting $\lambda := 2K^2(1+T)$ gives
\[
u_n(t) \;\leq\; \lambda \int_0^t u_n(s)\,ds, \qquad 0 \leq t \leq T .
\]
This is a Grönwall inequality with $g \equiv 0$ applied to a finite, non-negative function, so Lemma
\ref{lem:gronwall} of Appendix \ref{app:auxiliary} forces $u_n \equiv 0$. Hence
$Z_{t\wedge\tau_n} = 0$ almost surely for each $t$ and each $n$; letting $n \to \infty$ and using
$\tau_n \uparrow T$ together with the continuity of $Z$ gives $Z_t = 0$ almost surely for each fixed
$t \in [0,T]$.

It remains to upgrade this to indistinguishability, and the step is not vacuous: the null set so far
depends on $t$ (Remark \ref{rem:modification}). Let $\{r_n\}$ enumerate the rationals of $[0,T]$ and
let $\Omega_0$ be the intersection of the full-measure events $\{Z_{r_n}=0\}$ with the full-measure
event on which $Z$ has continuous paths. Then $\PP(\Omega_0)=1$, and for $\omega \in \Omega_0$
the function $t \mapsto Z_t(\omega)$ is continuous and vanishes on a dense set, hence vanishes
identically. Thus $X$ and $Y$ are indistinguishable. \hfill $\blacksquare$

\medskip

% \noindent The localisation in \eqref{eq:sdelocalising} is worth pausing over, since it is the
% argument Example \ref{example_stoppingTimes} was introduced to prepare. The pattern is invariably the
% same: one cannot integrate or take expectations in the class where the object naturally lives, so one
% stops the process at the first time it leaves a bounded region, works in the well-behaved class there,
% and lets the region exhaust the space. Without it the proof above would appeal to an isometry that
% does not hold for these integrands and to a Grönwall inequality that could be vacuous.

Three comments on the hypotheses, none of which is pedantry.

First, both conditions are needed, and they do different jobs: the Lipschitz condition delivers uniqueness, whereas the linear growth condition prevents the solution from exploding in finite time --- exactly as $\dot{y}=y^2$ does in the deterministic theory. If $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are globally Lipschitz in $x$ uniformly in $t$, and $|\mu(t,0)|$ and $|\sigma(t,0)|$ are bounded on $[0,T]$, then linear growth follows automatically, so the second hypothesis is often not stated separately; we state it because the multidimensional version, Theorem \ref{theorem:solutions:ndimensional_sde}, does.

Second, the square-integrability of the initial condition is not decoration. The whole construction of the Itô integral took place in $L^2$, and the Picard iterates are estimated there; without $\E[X_0^2]<\infty$ the very first iterate need not lie in the space where the argument is conducted.

Third, and most consequentially for what follows, the word ``unique'' conceals a distinction that this formulation is too coarse to see. We have asserted \emph{pathwise} uniqueness: given the Brownian motion, the solution is determined. One may instead ask only that the \emph{law} of the solution be determined, which is a weaker requirement and corresponds to the notion of a \emph{weak} solution --- one where the driving Brownian motion is part of the output rather than the input. The two notions genuinely differ, and the gap between them is not a curiosity: the Lipschitz hypothesis above is violated by one of the most widely used models in this dissertation, the square-root variance process of the Heston model of the next chapter (Subsection \ref{subsec:stochvol}), whose diffusion coefficient $\xi\sqrt{v}$ is only Hölder continuous of order $\tfrac12$ at the origin. Theorem \ref{thm:sdeexistence} as stated therefore does \emph{not} cover the Heston model. We now repair this.

\subsubsection*{Beyond Lipschitz: strong and weak solutions}

Let us first make the distinction precise, since it is systematically blurred in the applied 
literature and the blurring causes real confusion.

\begin{definition}[Strong and weak solutions]\label{def:strongweak}
Consider the equation $dX_t = \mu(t,X_t)\,dt + \sigma(t,X_t)\,dW_t$ with a given $\mathcal{F}_0$-measurable $X_0$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item[i)] A \textbf{strong solution} is a process $X$, adapted to the augmented filtration 
    generated by a \emph{given} Brownian motion $W$ and the initial condition, satisfying the 
    equation almost surely. \emph{Pathwise uniqueness} holds if any two strong solutions on the same 
    space, driven by the same $W$, are indistinguishable.
    \item[ii)] A \textbf{weak solution} is a pair $(X,W)$ together with a filtered probability space, 
    such that $W$ is a Brownian motion for that filtration and the equation holds. 
    \emph{Uniqueness in law} holds if any two weak solutions induce the same law on $C[0,T]$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

The distinction is one of information. For a strong solution the noise is handed to us and the 
trajectory is a measurable functional of it --- one may, at least in principle, simulate the 
solution from a given stream of random numbers. For a weak solution we are permitted to construct 
the noise ourselves, and all we control is the distribution of the result. For pricing this is often 
enough, since prices are expectations and expectations depend only on the law; for hedging, and for 
any pathwise statement, it is not.

The relation between the four notions is settled by the Yamada--Watanabe theorem: pathwise 
uniqueness together with existence of a weak solution implies existence of a strong solution and 
uniqueness in law. What we require is the companion criterion, which relaxes the Lipschitz condition 
in exactly the direction needed.

\begin{theorem}[Yamada--Watanabe]\label{thm:yamadawatanabe}
Consider the one-dimensional stochastic differential equation $dX_t = \mu(t,X_t)\,dt + \sigma(t,X_t)\,dW_t$. Suppose there
exist a strictly increasing function $\eta:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)$ with $\eta(0)=0$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:yamadacondition}
\int_{0^+} \frac{du}{\eta(u)^2} = \infty,
\end{equation}
and a strictly increasing concave $\zeta$ with $\zeta(0)=0$ and $\int_{0^+}du/\zeta(u)=\infty$,
such that for all $t$ and all $x,y$
\[
\bigl|\sigma(t,x)-\sigma(t,y)\bigr| \leq \eta\bigl(|x-y|\bigr), \qquad
\bigl|\mu(t,x)-\mu(t,y)\bigr| \leq \zeta\bigl(|x-y|\bigr).
\]
Then pathwise uniqueness holds for the equation \cite{yamada1971}. \hfill $\square$
\end{theorem}

The two moduli are written $\eta$ and $\zeta$ rather than the $h$ and $\varrho$ of the original
paper: the Latin letter is wanted elsewhere, and $\varrho$ sits one glyph away from the correlation
$\rho$, which is reserved throughout.
The point of \eqref{eq:yamadacondition} is that it admits $\eta(u) = u^{\gamma}$ for every
$\gamma \geq \tfrac12$, since $\int_{0^+} u^{-2\gamma}du$ diverges precisely when
$2\gamma \geq 1$. Hölder continuity of order $\tfrac12$ is thus permitted for the diffusion 
coefficient --- and only just, the exponent $\tfrac12$ being critical. The drift is treated 
separately and more leniently, needing only an Osgood-type modulus.

\begin{example}[The square-root process]\label{example:cirwellposed}
Consider the process which will serve as the variance in the Heston model of Chapter
\ref{ch:finance} (Subsection \ref{subsec:stochvol}),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cir}
dv_t = \kappa(\theta - v_t)\,dt + \xi\sqrt{v_t}\,dW_t, \qquad v_0 > 0,
\end{equation}
with $\kappa,\theta,\xi>0$. The drift is affine, hence Lipschitz. The diffusion coefficient 
$\sigma(v)=\xi\sqrt{v}$ is not Lipschitz at the origin, but it satisfies 
$|\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{y}| \leq \sqrt{|x-y|}$, so Theorem \ref{thm:yamadawatanabe} applies with
$\eta(u)=\xi\sqrt{u}$, and pathwise uniqueness holds. A non-negative solution exists, and 
\eqref{eq:cir} is therefore well posed --- but by Yamada--Watanabe, not by Theorem 
\ref{thm:sdeexistence}.
\end{example}

Well-posedness is not the end of the story for \eqref{eq:cir}, because we also need the solution to 
stay strictly positive: it is to be a variance, and $\sqrt{v_t}$ must make sense. Note that the 
diffusion coefficient vanishes at $v=0$ while the drift there equals $\kappa\theta > 0$, so the 
origin is a point where the noise switches off and the drift pushes upward. Whether that upward push 
is strong enough is a quantitative question with a sharp answer.

\begin{proposition}[Feller condition]\label{prop:feller}
Let $v$ solve \eqref{eq:cir} with $v_0>0$. If
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fellercondition}
2\kappa\theta \geq \xi^2,
\end{equation}
then $\PP(v_t > 0 \text{ for all } t \in [0,T]) = 1$. If $2\kappa\theta < \xi^2$, the process 
reaches $0$ with positive probability, though it is instantaneously reflecting and does not become 
negative \cite{feller1951}. \hfill $\square$
\end{proposition}

Condition \eqref{eq:fellercondition} has a transparent reading: mean reversion must be strong 
enough, and the volatility of volatility small enough, that the drift dominates the noise near the 
origin. It is worth knowing that in practice it is very often \emph{violated} by calibrated 
parameters --- fitting a steep short-maturity skew tends to demand a large $\xi$ --- and that this 
is a well-known source of difficulty in numerical work, since a naive Euler discretisation of 
\eqref{eq:cir} will produce negative values and then fail on $\sqrt{v_t}$. Practitioners resort to 
truncation or reflection schemes, or to exact simulation of the non-central $\chi^2$ transition law. 
We flag the point here because it is a genuine instance of the theory in this section constraining 
what can be done in Chapter \ref{ch:applications}, rather than a technicality to be waved through.

\subsection{The multidimensional case}\label{subsec:multidimensional}

% For the time being, we have only addressed processes in dimension 1. Almost every result already covered can be restated for multidimensional processes or has a direct analogue.

By a multidimensional Brownian motion on $\R^n$ we mean a stochastic process of the form  $\mathbf{W}(t) = (W_1(t),W_2(t),\dots,W_n(t))$ formed by $n$ independent Brownian motions on $\R$. Similarly, we can set them to start at $x_0\in \R^n$ just by a simple translation. It is a martingale and a Markov process that, starting at $\vec{0}$, has a transition density function given by $p_t(x) = (2\pi t)^{-n/2}e^{-\lVert x \rVert^2 / 2t}.$

There are certain properties of these processes that dramatically change with dimension. For example, for $n= 1$, it returns to 0 infinitely often, and for $n= 2$ enters arbitrarily small neighbourhoods of $0$ infinitely often with probability 1, however the probability that it reaches $\vec 0$ is 0. For $n\geq 3$, the process is \emph{transient}, satisfying $\lim_{t\rightarrow \infty } \lVert \mathbf{W}(t) \rVert = \infty$ almost surely. 

In terms of fractal dimensions, the \emph{range} $\mathbf{W}([0,t])$ has Hausdorff dimension $2$ for $n \geq 2$, and dimension $1$ for $n=1$ (where it is simply an interval). The \emph{graph} $\{(s,\mathbf{W}(s)) : s\in [0,t]\} \subset \R^{1+n}$ behaves differently: it has dimension $3/2$ for $n = 1$, a classical result of Taylor \cite{taylor1953}, and dimension $2$ for $n \geq 2$. These fascinating properties of Brownian motion can be found in \cite{falconer}.

The exponent $3/2$ in the one-dimensional graph is not an isolated curiosity. A graph of dimension $3/2$ is what one expects of a function which is $\alpha$-Hölder for every $\alpha < 1/2$ and for no $\alpha \geq 1/2$ --- the Hölder exponent bounds the dimension of the graph above by $2-\alpha$ --- and that critical exponent $1/2$ --- together with what happens on either side of it --- will return in Part \ref{part:part2} as the organising theme of the whole dissertation.

We can therefore define a multidimensional stochastic integral (expressed in differential form) as
\begin{equation}
    dX_t = \mu \, dt + \sum_{j=1}^{m} \sigma_j \, dW_t^{j}
\end{equation}
for an $m$-dimensional driver, each integral being considered separately. Let
\(W_1(t),\dots,W_m(t)\) be independent Brownian motions. Consider \(n\) Itô processes \(X_t^{(1)}, X_t^{(2)}, \ldots, X_t^{(n)}\) given by
\begin{equation}\label{equation:general_multidmensionalProcess}
X_t^{(i)} = X_0^{(i)} +  \int_0^t \mu_i(s) \, ds +\sum_{j=1}^m \int_0^t \sigma_{ij}(s) \, dW_j(s)  , \quad 1 \leq i \leq n,
\end{equation}
where \(\mu_{i} \in \mathcal{L}(\Omega, L^1[0,T])\) and \(\sigma_{ij} \in \mathcal{L}(\Omega, L^2[0,T])\) for all \(1 \leq i \leq n\) and \(1 \leq j \leq m\). Collecting $W = (W_1,\dots,W_m)\transp$ and
$X_t = (X^{(1)}_t,\dots,X^{(n)}_t)\transp$ into column vectors, $\mu = (\mu_1,\dots,\mu_n)\transp$
likewise, and $\sigma = [\sigma_{ij}]$ into an $n\times m$ matrix, equation
\eqref{equation:general_multidmensionalProcess} becomes
\[
X_t = X_0 + \int_0^t \mu(s)\,ds + \int_0^t \sigma(s)\,dW(s), \qquad 0 \leq t \leq T,
\]
the integrals being taken componentwise. Growth conditions on $\mu_i$ and $\sigma_{ij}$ must again be
imposed for the solution to make sense. Itô's formula holds as before, now accounting for the crossed
second derivatives; since every subsequent computation in this dissertation --- the Itô product rule,
the Black--Scholes derivation and the Heston system of the next chapter (Subsections
\ref{subsec:bs_model} and \ref{subsec:stochvol}) and the proof of Girsanov's theorem in Appendix
\ref{app:proof:girsanov} ---
rests on it, we state it explicitly --- but one ingredient has to be supplied first.

So far, a multidimensional Brownian motion has \emph{independent} components, and both the formula
and the multiplication table that accompanies it presuppose an object the definition above does not
provide. In modelling one almost always wants the components to be
correlated --- a falling equity price accompanied by rising volatility, two rates moving together,
an exchange rate and the foreign interest rate --- and we shall shortly make that precise.

Constructing such an object requires a way of \emph{recognising} a Brownian motion, and the
following classical theorem supplies one. We state it here, rather than alongside the change-of-measure
machinery where it is used a second time, because it is needed already in the construction below.

\begin{theorem}[Lévy's Characterisation Theorem]\label{thm:levycharacterisation}
    Let $X$ be a continuous local martingale on a filtered probability space $\FPSpace$ with $X_0 = 0$ and $\langle X \rangle_t = t$ almost surely for every $t \in [0,T]$. Then $X$ is an $\Filt$-Brownian motion under $\PP$.

    More generally, let $X = (X^1,\dots,X^n)$ be a vector of continuous local martingales with $X_0 = 0$ and
    \[
    \langle X^i, X^j \rangle_t = \delta_{ij}\,t \qquad \text{almost surely, for all } i,j \text{ and all } t,
    \]
    where $\delta_{ij}$ is the Kronecker delta. Then $X$ is an $n$-dimensional $\Filt$-Brownian motion with independent components.
\end{theorem}
\textbf{\textit{Proof:}} The argument is given in Appendix \ref{app:proof:levy}; see also \cite[Thm.\ 8.4.2]{kuo2006}. \hfill $\blacksquare$

The statement deserves to be read slowly, because it is considerably stronger than it looks. Being a Brownian motion is, by Definition \ref{def:Brownian-motion}, a statement about the \emph{joint law} of the whole family $\{X_t\}$: independent, stationary, Gaussian increments. Lévy's theorem says that all of this is already implied by two facts about second moments --- that $X$ is a local martingale and that its quadratic variation is exactly $t$. Gaussianity is not assumed anywhere; it is a conclusion.

The multidimensional form, with the condition $\langle X^i,X^j\rangle_t = \delta_{ij}t$, is the one we shall actually need, and it will be used twice. Immediately below it produces a correlated Brownian motion from an independent one. Later, in the proof of Girsanov's theorem, it will allow us to recognise a process as a Brownian motion under a \emph{new} measure without recomputing any distributions --- one need only check that quadratic variation is preserved, which it is, since quadratic variation is a pathwise notion and equivalent measures agree on which paths occur.

With the criterion in hand we may define the object we want.

\begin{definition}[Correlated Brownian motion]\label{def:correlatedbm}
Let $P = (\rho_{ij}) \in \R^{n\times n}$ be symmetric and positive semi-definite with 
$\rho_{ii} = 1$ for all $i$. An $n$-dimensional process 
$\widetilde{\mathbf{W}} = (\widetilde W^1, \dots, \widetilde W^n)$ is a \textbf{Brownian motion with 
correlation matrix $P$} if each $\widetilde W^i$ is a one-dimensional Brownian motion and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:correlatedbm}
\bigl\langle \widetilde W^i, \widetilde W^j \bigr\rangle_t = \rho_{ij}\,t, \qquad 1 \leq i,j \leq n, \quad t \in [0,T].
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

Such a process always exists, and is obtained from an independent one by a linear map. Since $P$ is 
symmetric positive semi-definite it admits a factorisation $P = LL\transp$ --- the Cholesky 
factorisation when $P$ is positive definite, and a square root more generally. Setting 
$\widetilde{\mathbf{W}} := L\,\mathbf{W}$ for an independent $\mathbf{W}$, bilinearity of the 
quadratic covariation \eqref{eq:polarisation} gives
\[
\bigl\langle \widetilde W^i, \widetilde W^j \bigr\rangle_t 
= \sum_{k,l} L_{ik}L_{jl} \bigl\langle W^k, W^l \bigr\rangle_t 
= \sum_{k} L_{ik}L_{jk}\,t = (LL\transp)_{ij}\,t = \rho_{ij}\,t,
\]
and each component is a continuous local martingale with $\langle \widetilde W^i\rangle_t = t$, hence 
a Brownian motion by Theorem \ref{thm:levycharacterisation}.

% The positive semi-definiteness of $P$ is a genuine constraint and not a formality. It is what makes 
% the factorisation possible, and it is a real restriction as soon as $n \geq 3$: one cannot 
% independently choose $\rho_{12}$, $\rho_{13}$ and $\rho_{23}$, since a strongly positive correlation 
% between the first two pairs forbids a strongly negative one between the third. This is a familiar 
% nuisance in practice, where correlation matrices estimated entrywise from market data frequently 
% fail to be positive semi-definite and must be repaired before they can be used.

With this in hand we may state the formula.

\begin{theorem}[Multidimensional Itô formula]\label{thm:itomulti}
Let $X$ be the $n$-dimensional Itô process of
\eqref{equation:general_multidmensionalProcess}, driven by an $m$-dimensional Brownian motion
with independent components, and let
$V \in \mathcal{C}^{1,2}\bigl([0,T]\times\R^n,\R\bigr)$.
Then, almost surely and for every $t \in [0,T]$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:itomulti}
dV(t,X_t) = \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}\,dt
 + \sum_{l=1}^{n}\frac{\partial V}{\partial x_l}\,dX^{(l)}_t
 + \frac12 \sum_{l,h=1}^{n} \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x_l \partial x_h}\,d\bigl\langle X^{(l)},X^{(h)}\bigr\rangle_t ,
\end{equation}
where the cross-variations are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:crossvariation}
d\bigl\langle X^{(l)},X^{(h)}\bigr\rangle_t
 = \sum_{j=1}^{m}\sigma_{lj}(t)\sigma_{hj}(t)\,dt
 = \bigl(\sigma\sigma\transp\bigr)_{lh}\,dt .
\end{equation}
If instead the driving Brownian motion has correlation matrix $P = (\rho_{jk})$ in the sense of
Definition \ref{def:correlatedbm}, \eqref{eq:itomulti} is unchanged and
\eqref{eq:crossvariation} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:crossvariationcorrelated}
d\bigl\langle X^{(l)},X^{(h)}\bigr\rangle_t
 = \sum_{j,k=1}^{m}\sigma_{lj}(t)\,\rho_{jk}\,\sigma_{hk}(t)\,dt
 = \bigl(\sigma P \sigma\transp\bigr)_{lh}\,dt .
\end{equation}
\qedopen
\end{theorem}

The proof is the one-dimensional argument of Theorem \ref{theorem:itorule} applied coordinatewise,
with the multiplication table of Table \ref{tab:ItoTable} supplying the second-order terms; see
\cite[Ch.\ 7]{kuo2006} or \cite[Ch.\ 4]{oksendal2003}. Taking $V(x_1,x_2) = x_1x_2$ and $n=2$
recovers the Itô product rule.

The correlated form \eqref{eq:crossvariationcorrelated} is stated because it, and not
\eqref{eq:crossvariation}, is what the Heston system \eqref{eq:heston} requires: that model is
specified with $d\langle \Wt^{\Spot},\Wt^{v}\rangle_t = \rho\,dt$, so its drivers are correlated by
construction. Nothing new is involved --- one may equally decorrelate first, writing
$\widetilde{\mathbf{W}} = L\mathbf{W}$ with $LL\transp = P$ as in Definition
\ref{def:correlatedbm}, and then apply \eqref{eq:crossvariation} to $\sigma L$ --- but keeping the
general form to hand avoids performing that substitution silently every time the table is used.

The Itô multiplication table extends accordingly.

\[
\begin{array}{c|ccccc}
\times & dt & dW_t^1 & dW_t^2 & \cdots & dW_t^n \\ \hline
dt & 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
dW_t^1 & 0 & dt & \rho_{12}\,dt & \cdots & \rho_{1n}\,dt \\
dW_t^2 & 0 & \rho_{21}\,dt & dt & \cdots & \rho_{2n}\,dt \\
\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
dW_t^n & 0 & \rho_{n1}\,dt & \rho_{n2}\,dt & \cdots & dt
\end{array}
\]
{\captionof{table}{Itô multiplication table for correlated $n$-dimensional Brownian motion.}%
\label{tab:ItoTable}}

The entries are not a new convention but a restatement of \eqref{eq:correlatedbm}: the symbol 
$dW^i_t\,dW^j_t$ abbreviates $d\langle W^i,W^j\rangle_t = \rho_{ij}\,dt$. The independent case is 
recovered by taking $P = \mathbb{I}_{n\times n}$.

\begin{example}
    
Let $V : \R^n \rightarrow \R $ be a  $\mathcal{C}^2$ function and let  $\nabla V = (\frac{\partial  V}{ \partial x_1}, \frac{\partial  V}{ \partial x_2}, \dots, \frac{\partial  V}{ \partial x_n})$ and $\Delta V = \sum_{i= 1}^n \frac {\partial^2 V}{\partial x_ i ^2}  $ to obtain

$$
V(\mathbf{W}(t)) = V(\mathbf{W}(0)) + \int_0^t \nabla V (\mathbf{W}(s)) \cdot d\mathbf{W}_s + \frac{1}{2}\int_0 ^t \Delta V (\mathbf{W}(s)) \, ds.
$$
This equation exhibits the deep connection between Brownian motion and diffusions, showing how \emph{one half of the Laplace operator} is the generator of Brownian motion; see Subsection \ref{section:itoDiff}.

% where the same $\tfrac12$ reappears from the semigroup side. The factor is not decorative: it is the quadratic variation $\langle W^i \rangle_t = t$ entering through the second-order term of Itô's rule, and it is the reason the heat equation associated with Brownian motion is $\partial_t u = \tfrac12 \Delta u$ rather than $\partial_t u = \Delta u$.

\end{example}
\begin{example}
    An important example is given by $V(x,y) = xy.$ Clearly, $\partial V / \partial x = y$ and also $\partial V / \partial y = x$. Therefore, if we have processes $X_t$ and $Z_t$, we obtain
    \begin{equation}
        d(X_t\,Z_t) = Z_t\,dX_t + X_t\,dZ_t + \frac 1 2 dX_t\, dZ_t+ \frac 1 2 dX_t\, dZ_t=Z_t\,dX_t + X_t\,dZ_t + dX_t\, dZ_t
    \end{equation}
    which is usually known as the Itô product formula, understanding $dX_t\,dZ_t$ as in Table \ref{tab:ItoTable}.
\end{example}

The following generalisation holds.

\begin{theorem} \label{theorem:solutions:ndimensional_sde}
    Let $\sigma(t,x)$ be an $n\times m$ matrix-valued function and $\mu(t,x)$ an $\R^n$-valued function. Assume there exists $C>0$ such that for all $t\in[0,T]$ and $x,y \in \R^n$, 
    $$
    \lVert \sigma (t,x) - \sigma(t,y) \rVert \leq C | x - y| \text{ } \text{ , } \text{ } |\mu(t,x) - \mu(t,y)| \leq C|x-y|
    $$
    and 
    $$
    \lVert \sigma(t,x)\rVert ^2 \leq C(1+|x|^2 ) \text{ } \text{ , } \text{ } |\mu(t,x)| ^2 \leq C(1+|x|^2 ) 
    $$ 
    where $\lVert \sigma \rVert^2 = \sum_i \sum_j \sigma_{ij}^2$. For any $\mathcal{F}_0$-measurable random vector $X_0$ of finite variance, the SDE $dX_t = \mu(t,X_t) \, dt +  \sigma(t,X_t) \, dW_t$ has a unique continuous solution. \hfill $\square$
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Probability measure changes}\label{subsec:measurechanges}

Another important topic we must cover has to do with probability changes. Brownian motion depends crucially on the underlying probability $\PP$. Given a process $X_t$, it is natural to ask whether there is a probability $\QQ$ so that $X_t$ is a Brownian motion with respect to $\QQ$. We assume the $\sigma$-algebra and the filtration remain unchanged.

The change of measure itself is engineered through a single family of processes, which we now introduce.

\begin{definition}[Exponential Process]
    Let $\alpha \in \mathcal{L}(\Omega, L^2[0,T])$. We define its exponential process by
    \begin{equation}\label{exponential_process_definition}
        \mathcal E _\alpha(t) = e^{  \int_0^t \alpha(s) dW(s)-\frac 1 2 \int_0^t \alpha^2(s)ds}
    \end{equation}
    which is precisely the solution of $d\mathcal E_\alpha(t) =\alpha(t)\mathcal{E}_\alpha (t) dW(t)$, $\mathcal E_\alpha(0) = 1$ so that $\mathcal{E}_\alpha(t)$ is a local martingale.
\end{definition}

This exponential process is important because it leads to the construction of a new probability $\QQ$ for which $\mathcal{E}_\alpha(t)$ is actually a martingale.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:exponentialmartingale}
    Let $\alpha \in \mathcal{L}(\Omega, L^2[0,T])$. Then $\mathcal E_\alpha(t)$ is a martingale if, and only if, $\E[\mathcal E_\alpha (t)]= 1 $ for all $t\in [0,T].$
\end{theorem}
\textbf{\textit{Proof:}} Necessity is immediate: if $\mathcal{E}_\alpha$ is a martingale then
$\E[\mathcal{E}_\alpha(t)] = \E\bigl[\E[\mathcal{E}_\alpha(t)\mid\mathcal{F}_0]\bigr]
= \E[\mathcal{E}_\alpha(0)] = 1$ for every $t$.

For sufficiency, recall from the definition that
$\mathcal{E}_\alpha(t) = 1 + \int_0^t \alpha(s)\mathcal{E}_\alpha(s)\,dW_s$, so $\mathcal{E}_\alpha$ is
a non-negative local martingale, and let $(\tau_n)$ be a localising sequence. A non-negative local
martingale is always a supermartingale --- apply the conditional Fatou lemma (Proposition
\ref{prop:condfatou}) to $\mathcal{E}_\alpha(t\wedge\tau_n) \to \mathcal{E}_\alpha(t)$ --- so for
$s<t$,
\[
\E\bigl[\mathcal{E}_\alpha(t) \mid \mathcal{F}_s\bigr] \leq \mathcal{E}_\alpha(s)
\qquad \text{almost surely.}
\]
Both sides are non-negative and, by hypothesis, have the same expectation, namely $1$. A
non-negative random variable dominated by another of equal expectation is almost surely equal to it,
so the inequality is an equality and $\mathcal{E}_\alpha$ is a martingale. See
\cite[Thm.\ 8.7.3]{kuo2006}. \hfill $\blacksquare$

The criterion just proved is exact, but it is not one that can be checked directly: verifying $\E[\mathcal E _\alpha (t)]= 1$ is very nearly as hard as verifying the martingale property itself. What is wanted is a condition on $\alpha$ alone, and the following supplies one.

\begin{theorem}[Novikov's condition]\label{thm:novikov}
    Let $\alpha \in \mathcal{L}(\Omega, L^2[0,T])$. If $\E\left[ e^{\frac 1 2 \int_0^T \alpha^2(s) ds} \right] < \infty$, then $\mathcal E_\alpha(t)$ is a martingale. A proof may be found in \cite[Sec.~8.7]{kuo2006}. \hfill $\square$

\end{theorem}

This is a much more practical way to check whether $\mathcal E_\alpha(t)$ is a martingale, and therefore whether we can construct a new probability $\QQ$ such that $d\QQ = \mathcal E_\alpha(T)\, d\PP$. For the following Theorem, we need to make use of two auxiliary lemmas.

\begin{lemma}[Conditional expectation under a change of measure]\label{lem:condexpchange}
    Let $\gamma$ be a nonnegative integrable random variable, so that $d\QQ = \gamma\, d \PP$ defines a new probability measure (see \eqref{eq:radonnikodym}). Then, for any $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{G} \subset \mathcal{F}$ and $Y$ such that $\E_{\QQ}[|Y|] < \infty$, it follows that
    \begin{equation}
        \E_{\QQ}[Y \,|\, \mathcal G] = \frac{\E_{\PP}[\gamma Y  \,|\, \mathcal G] }{\E_{\PP}[\gamma \,|\, \mathcal G] }, \, \, \QQ\text{-almost surely.}
    \end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\textbf{\textit{Proof:}} Integrability holds because
$\E_\PP[|Y|\gamma] = \E_\QQ[|Y|] < \infty$, so
$\E_\PP[\gamma Y\mid\mathcal{G}]$ is defined. Fix $G \in \mathcal{G}$. By the
defining property of conditional expectation under $\PP$ and the definition of $\QQ$,
\[
\int_G \E_\PP[\gamma Y \mid \mathcal{G}]\,d\PP
= \int_G \gamma Y\,d\PP = \int_G Y \,d\QQ
= \int_G \E_\QQ[Y\mid\mathcal{G}]\,d\QQ.
\]
On the other hand, writing $d\QQ = \gamma\,d\PP$ and using that
$\E_\QQ[Y\mid\mathcal{G}]$ is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable, so that it may be taken
out of a conditional expectation given $\mathcal{G}$ (Proposition
\ref{prop:condexpproperties}, item 4),
\[
\int_G \E_\QQ[Y\mid\mathcal{G}]\,d\QQ
= \int_G \E_\QQ[Y\mid\mathcal{G}]\,\gamma\,d\PP
= \int_G \E_\QQ[Y\mid\mathcal{G}]\;\E_\PP[\gamma\mid\mathcal{G}]\,d\PP.
\]
The two displays agree for every $G \in \mathcal{G}$, and both integrands are
$\mathcal{G}$-measurable, so they coincide almost surely. Dividing by
$\E_\PP[\gamma\mid\mathcal{G}]$, which is strictly positive $\QQ$-almost
surely, gives the result \cite[Lemma 8.9.2]{kuo2006}. \hfill $\blacksquare$

Then, if $\alpha \in \mathcal{L}(\Omega, L^2[0,T])$ satisfies the condition on Theorem \ref{thm:exponentialmartingale}, it follows that $\mathcal E_\alpha(t)$ is a $\PP$-martingale. Let $\QQ$ be defined by $d\QQ = \mathcal E_\alpha(T)\,d\PP$, that is, $\QQ(A) = \int_A \mathcal E_\alpha (T) \, d\PP \text{ for every  } A\in \mathcal F$.

\begin{lemma}[Martingale transfer]\label{lem:qmartingale}
Let $\alpha \in \mathcal{L}(\Omega, L^2[0,T])$.   Then a $\{\mathcal F_t \}$-adapted process $X_t$, $t\in[0,T]$ is a $\QQ$-martingale if, and only if, $X(t) \mathcal E_\alpha(t)$  is a $\PP$-martingale.
\end{lemma}
\textbf{\textit{Proof:}} Apply Lemma \ref{lem:condexpchange} with $\gamma = \mathcal{E}_\alpha(T)$ and
$\mathcal{G} = \mathcal{F}_s$. Since $\mathcal{E}_\alpha$ is a $\PP$-martingale,
$\E_\PP[\mathcal{E}_\alpha(T)\mid\mathcal{F}_s] = \mathcal{E}_\alpha(s)$, so for
$s \leq t$
\begin{equation}
\E_\QQ\bigl[X(t) \mid \mathcal{F}_s\bigr]
= \frac{\E_\PP\bigl[X(t)\mathcal{E}_\alpha(T)\mid\mathcal{F}_s\bigr]}{\mathcal{E}_\alpha(s)} .
\end{equation}
The numerator simplifies by conditioning on $\mathcal{F}_t$ first and using that $X(t)$ is
$\mathcal{F}_t$-measurable:
\[
\E_\PP\bigl[X(t)\mathcal{E}_\alpha(T)\mid\mathcal{F}_s\bigr]
= \E_\PP\Bigl[X(t)\,\E_\PP[\mathcal{E}_\alpha(T)\mid\mathcal{F}_t]\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_s\Bigr]
= \E_\PP\bigl[X(t)\mathcal{E}_\alpha(t)\mid\mathcal{F}_s\bigr].
\]
Hence $\E_\QQ[X(t)\mid\mathcal{F}_s] = X(s)$ if and only if
$\E_\PP[X(t)\mathcal{E}_\alpha(t)\mid\mathcal{F}_s] = X(s)\mathcal{E}_\alpha(s)$,
which is the assertion \cite[Lemma 8.9.3]{kuo2006}. \hfill $\blacksquare$

Finally, the following key result holds from Lemmas \ref{lem:condexpchange} and \ref{lem:qmartingale}.

\begin{theorem}[Girsanov's Theorem]\label{girsanovtheorem}
Let $\alpha \in \mathcal{L}(\Omega, L^2[0,T])$ such that $\mathcal E_\alpha$ is a martingale. Then the process 

\begin{equation}
    Z(t) = W(t) - \int_0^t \alpha(s) \, ds, \text{ }\text{ } t\in[0,T]
\end{equation}
is a Brownian motion with respect to the measure $d\QQ  = \mathcal E_\alpha (T) d\PP$.
\end{theorem}
\textbf{\textit{Proof:}} The argument is given in Appendix \ref{app:proof:girsanov}; see also \cite[Thm.\ 8.9.4]{kuo2006}. \hfill $\blacksquare$

Girsanov's theorem is the formal statement of something we shall use constantly: \emph{changing the 
measure changes the drift and leaves the volatility alone}. The Brownian motion $Z$ under 
$\QQ$ is the old $W$ with a drift subtracted, and nothing else has moved. The reason is 
already contained in Theorem \ref{thm:levycharacterisation}: to verify that $Z$ is a $\QQ$-Brownian 
motion one need only check that it is a continuous local martingale with $\langle Z\rangle_t = t$, 
and quadratic variation is a pathwise limit, so it is unaffected by passing to an equivalent 
measure. Two equivalent measures disagree about how likely a path is, never about which paths 
exist. This observation is the mathematical content of the statement --- which we shall meet again 
in the next chapter, in Section \ref{sec:blackscholes} --- that an option's price does not depend on the expected return 
of the underlying, while it depends critically on its volatility.

For the multi-factor models of the next chapter (Section \ref{sec:othermodels}) we require the vector-valued form.

\begin{theorem}[Girsanov's Theorem, multidimensional]\label{thm:girsanovmulti}
Let $\mathbf{W} = (W^1,\dots,W^n)$ be an $n$-dimensional Brownian motion and let 
$\alpha = (\alpha^1,\dots,\alpha^n)$ be an adapted process with 
$\int_0^T \lVert\alpha(s)\rVert^2\,ds < \infty$ almost surely. Define
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{E}_\alpha(t) = \exp\left( \int_0^t \alpha(s)\transp\,d\mathbf{W}(s) - \frac{1}{2}\int_0^t \lVert \alpha(s)\rVert^2\,ds \right),
\end{equation}
and suppose Novikov's condition holds in the form 
$\E\bigl[\exp\bigl(\tfrac12\int_0^T \lVert\alpha(s)\rVert^2 ds\bigr)\bigr] < \infty$. Then 
$\mathcal{E}_\alpha$ is a martingale, $d\QQ = \mathcal{E}_\alpha(T)\,d\PP$ defines a 
probability measure equivalent to $\PP$, and
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Z}(t) = \mathbf{W}(t) - \int_0^t \alpha(s)\,ds, \qquad t \in [0,T],
\end{equation}
is an $n$-dimensional $\QQ$-Brownian motion with independent components. \hfill $\square$
\end{theorem}

That the components of $\mathbf{Z}$ remain independent under $\QQ$ deserves emphasis, since 
it is not obvious and is exactly what the multidimensional Lévy characterisation delivers: the 
cross-variations $\langle Z^i, Z^j\rangle_t = \delta_{ij}t$ are pathwise quantities and survive the 
change of measure untouched. Note also that this is a statement about \emph{independent} driving 
factors; when a model is specified with correlated Brownian motions, as the Heston model of 
Subsection \ref{subsec:stochvol} is, one applies the theorem after decorrelating, in the manner of 
Definition \ref{def:correlatedbm} above.

\subsubsection*{The Martingale Representation Theorem}

Girsanov's theorem tells us which measures are available. The following tells us which \emph{payoffs} 
are attainable, and it is the deepest result of this subsection. Together the two constitute the 
entire mathematical content of the fundamental theorems of asset pricing, proved in Section
\ref{sec:derivatives} of the next chapter.

\begin{theorem}[Martingale representation, Itô]\label{thm:mrt}
Let $\mathbf{W}$ be an $n$-dimensional Brownian motion on $\PSpace$ and let
$\FiltW$ be its augmented natural filtration. Let $M$ be a square-integrable
$\PP$-martingale with respect to it. Then there exists an adapted process
$\pi = (\pi^1,\dots,\pi^n) \in L^2_{\mathrm{ad}}([0,T]\times\Omega)$, unique up to
$dt\otimes d\PP$-null sets, such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mrt}
M_t = M_0 + \int_0^t \pi(s)\transp \, d\mathbf{W}(s), \qquad t \in [0,T],
\end{equation}
almost surely. If $M$ is only a local martingale, the same holds with $\pi$ in the larger class 
$\mathcal{L}(\Omega,L^2[0,T])$. \hfill $\square$
\end{theorem}

The theorem should be read as a converse to Theorem \ref{theorem:martingale_property}. We 
showed that every Itô integral is a martingale; here we learn that, provided the filtration is the 
one generated by $\mathbf{W}$ and nothing more, \emph{every} martingale is an Itô integral. In the 
language of Proposition \ref{prop:condjensenl2}, item ii), the stochastic integrals do not 
merely sit inside the space of martingales: they exhaust it.



Two aspects of the hypothesis deserve to be underlined, because both will be load-bearing. The first is that the filtration must be generated by the Brownian motion itself. If 
$\Filt$ carries additional information --- like another independent Brownian motion or an independent random variable revealed at time $T$ --- the conclusion fails 
outright, and it fails for an obvious reason: a martingale driven by that extra randomness cannot be 
written as an integral against $\mathbf{W}$, because the integral would be measurable with respect 
to the wrong $\sigma$-algebra: \emph{no more randomness 
in the model than the driving Brownian motion supplies}.

The second is that $\pi$ is not constructed. The proof is an orthogonality argument: one shows 
that the stochastic integrals form a closed subspace of $L^2(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_T)$ whose orthogonal 
complement contains only constants, using the fact that exponential martingales 
$\mathcal{E}_\alpha(T)$ span a dense set. Existence is therefore obtained without any formula for 
$\pi$, which is worth knowing in advance: the theorem will guarantee that a replicating strategy 
exists while saying nothing whatever about how to compute it. Recovering $\pi$ in concrete cases 
is precisely the delta-hedging computation of Section \ref{sec:blackscholes}, and in models where no 
closed form is available it is a numerical problem.

% We can already state the consequence for which we shall need it, though the vocabulary is only 
% introduced in the next section.

\begin{remark}[Why this matters for pricing]\label{rem:mrtpricing}
Let $\Payoff_T$ be a square-integrable contingent claim and let $\QQ$ be an equivalent 
martingale measure. The process 
$M_t := \Ep{\QQ}[\Payoff_T/S^0_T \mid \mathcal{F}_t]$ is by construction a 
$\QQ$-martingale. If the filtration is generated 
by the Brownian motion driving the traded assets, Theorem \ref{thm:mrt} applied under $\QQ$ 
produces $\pi$ with $M_t = M_0 + \int_0^t \pi\transp\,d\mathbf{Z}$, and $\pi$ is
--- after rewriting the integral in terms of the discounted asset prices --- a self-financing
strategy replicating $\Payoff_T$ exactly. The letter is chosen deliberately: $\pi$ denotes a
trading strategy throughout Chapter \ref{ch:finance}, and the integrand supplied by Theorem
\ref{thm:mrt} is precisely that.

One hypothesis is being used silently here and is worth naming. Theorem \ref{thm:mrt} is applied under $\QQ$ to the $\QQ$-Brownian motion $\mathbf{Z}$, so
what it requires is that the filtration be generated by $\mathbf{Z}$, whereas the filtration we
have is the one generated by $\mathbf{W}$. The two coincide: by Theorem \ref{thm:girsanovmulti},
$\mathbf{Z}_t = \mathbf{W}_t - \int_0^t\alpha\,ds$ with $\alpha$ adapted, so each is a measurable
functional of the other and, after the augmentation of Convention \ref{conv:usualconditions},
$\FiltW = (\mathcal{F}^{\mathbf{Z}}_t)_{t\in[0,T]}$. Girsanov changes the measure and leaves the
information alone, which is exactly what makes the two theorems composable.

% Every claim is thus attainable, so the market is \emph{complete}, and the price of the claim is not 
% merely a plausible expectation but the unique cost of a portfolio that reproduces it in every state 
% of the world. This is the substance behind the Second Fundamental Theorem 
% (Theorem \ref{thm:secondFTAP}), and it is the reason the Black--Scholes model is complete 
% (Section \ref{sec:blackscholes}) while the stochastic volatility models of Subsection 
% \ref{subsec:stochvol} are not: in the latter, the filtration is generated by \emph{two} Brownian 
% motions while only \emph{one} asset is traded, the hypothesis of Theorem \ref{thm:mrt} fails, and 
% the volatility risk cannot be hedged away.

% One word on function spaces, since two appear. Completeness will be defined below by quantifying over
% $L^\infty(\mathcal{F}_T)$, which is the Delbaen--Schachermayer convention and the right one for the
% Fundamental Theorems: boundedness is what makes the separating-hyperplane argument work in a dual
% pair. The Martingale Representation Theorem, by contrast, is an $L^2$ statement, because it is

\end{remark}

\subsection{Itô Diffusions}\label{section:itoDiff}

Recall that, for a Markov process, the \textit{transition probability} $P_{s,x}(t,A) = \PP(X_t \in A \, | \, X_s = x)$, for $A$ measurable, $0\leq s\leq t \leq T$ and $x\in \R^n$. The Chapman--Kolmogorov equation states that 
$$
P_{s,x}(t,A) = \int_{\R^n} P_{u,z}(t,A) \, P_{s,x}(u,dz).
$$

\begin{definition}[Diffusion Process]\label{def:diffusion}
    A $\R^n$-valued process $X_t$, $t\in [0,T]$ is called a \textbf{diffusion process} if its transition probabilities $\{P_{s,x}(t, \cdot )\}$ satisfy the following three conditions for every $t\in [0,T]$, every $x\in\R^n$ and every $\delta > 0$:

     \begin{equation*}
                 \text{i) }   \lim_ {\varepsilon \rightarrow 0^+ }\frac{1}{\varepsilon }  \int_ {\{\|y-x\|>\delta\}} P_{t,x}(t+\varepsilon,dy) = 0             \end{equation*}

                  \begin{equation*}
                 \text{ii) }   \lim_ {\varepsilon \rightarrow 0^+ }\frac{1}{\varepsilon }  \int_ {\{\|y-x\|\leq\delta\}} (y-x) \, P_{t,x}(t+\varepsilon,dy) = \mu(t,x) \in \R^n \text{ exists,}\end{equation*}

                        \begin{equation*}
                 \text{iii) }   \lim_ {\varepsilon \rightarrow 0^+ }\frac{1}{\varepsilon }  \int_ {\{\|y-x\|\leq\delta\}}  (y-x)(y-x)\transp  \, P_{t,x}(t+\varepsilon,dy) = \Sigma(t,x) \in \R^{n\times n} \text{ exists,}\end{equation*}

\noindent where the vectors in $\R^n$ are implicitly taken as columns, so that $(y-x)(y-x)\transp$ yields a symmetric positive semi-definite matrix. $\mu$ and $\Sigma$ are called the \textit{drift} and the \textit{diffusion} coefficient, respectively.
\end{definition}

% The restriction of the domain of integration to a ball around $x$ is essential and not a technicality: the integral of a probability measure over the whole of $\R^n$ is $1$, so dropping the constraint in i) would make the limit $+\infty$ rather than $0$.

Read correctly, the three conditions say that in a short time $\varepsilon$ the process moves a distance of order $\sqrt{\varepsilon}$ with overwhelming probability, that the mean of that displacement is of order $\varepsilon$, and that its covariance is of order $\varepsilon$ as well. When conditions ii) and iii) hold, the truncation at level $\delta$ turns out not to affect the limits, so we may and shall write them informally as
\[
\mu(t,x) = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0^+}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\E\left[X_{t+\varepsilon}-x \mid X_t = x\right], \qquad
\Sigma(t,x) = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0^+}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\E\left[(X_{t+\varepsilon}-x)(X_{t+\varepsilon}-x)\transp \mid X_t = x\right].
\]

Note how condition i) forbids the process from having jumps: it is precisely the statement that a displacement of size $\delta$ in time $\varepsilon$ has probability $o(\varepsilon)$. Therefore the Poisson process, for which such a displacement has probability $\lambda\varepsilon + o(\varepsilon)$, is not a diffusion --- which is the promise made after Example \ref{example:randomwalk}, that the Poisson process would reappear as the canonical process that is \emph{not} one.

\begin{example}\label{example:bmisdiffusion}
    Take an $\R^n$-valued Brownian motion $W_t$. Note that $\lVert W_t\rVert^2$ is a sum of $n$ independent squares of normal variables, so that $\E\lVert W_\varepsilon\rVert^4 = n(n+2)\varepsilon^2$. Hence, for any $c>0$, Markov's inequality applied to the fourth power gives
    \[
    \PP\bigl(\lVert W_{t+\varepsilon}-x\rVert\geq c \,\big|\, W_t=x\bigr)
    = \PP\bigl(\lVert W_{t+\varepsilon}-W_t\rVert \geq c\bigr)
    \leq c^{-4}\,\E\lVert W_\varepsilon\rVert^4
    = c^{-4}n(n+2)\varepsilon^2 ,
    \]
    which is $o(\varepsilon)$; taking $c = \delta$ gives condition i) of Definition \ref{def:diffusion} directly. It is therefore satisfied, and a direct computation of the remaining two limits gives $\mu(t,x) = 0$ and $\Sigma(t,x)= \mathbb{I}_{n\times n}$.
\end{example}

The following result finally connects the previous concepts and gives an unambiguous meaning to $\mu.$

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:sdeisdiffusion}
    Let $\sigma(t,x)$ and $\mu(t,x)$ be functions as in Theorem \ref{theorem:solutions:ndimensional_sde}. Assume they are also continuous on $[0,T] \times \R^n$. The solution to the SDE there defined is a diffusion process where $\mu(t,x)$ is identified and $\Sigma(t,x)= \sigma(t,x)\,\sigma(t,x)\transp$. \hfill $\square$
\end{theorem}

The proof of this Theorem is beyond the scope of our purposes and can be found in \cite{kuo2006}.

We have reached a critical point where diffusions, SDEs and semigroups intersect. These seemingly unrelated mathematical objects give rise to a set of (parabolic) partial differential equations (\textit{PDEs}) that are crucial for computational purposes and pricing financial derivatives. In this context, powerful mathematical tools can be applied.

The first of the approaches, where we consider a semigroup of operators, is included for completeness purposes, showing how its infinitesimal generators may be used to construct diffusion processes. It is also included to illustrate how Functional Analysis arises in this context.
\vspace{0.3cm}

\textbf{\underline{1. Semigroup approach}}
\vspace{0.2cm}

We restrict to the \textbf{stationary case}, in which $\mu$ and $\Sigma$ depend on $x$ alone, and use
standard Banach-space language; see \cite{brezis2011}.

A family $\{P_t\}_{t\geq0}$ of linear operators on a Banach space $\mathbb{B}$ is a
\textbf{contraction semigroup} if $P_0 = \mathrm{I}$ and $P_{t+s}=P_tP_s$ (semigroup property),
$P_t v \to v$ as $t \downarrow 0$ for every $v$ (strong continuity), and
$\lVert P_t v\rVert \leq \lVert v\rVert$ (contraction). The letter is the one already used for
transition probabilities in Subsection \ref{subsec:markovprocesses}, and deliberately not $T$, which
is the maturity. Its \textbf{infinitesimal generator} is
\begin{equation}\label{pre_hilleYoshida}
\mathcal{A}(v) = \lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}\bigl(P_t(v)-v\bigr),
\end{equation}
defined on those $v$ for which the limit exists strongly; it is typically unbounded, as already for
the translation semigroup $P_t f(x) = f(x+t)$ on $C_0[0,\infty)$, whose generator is $\mathcal{A}f = f'$.
The choice of space is not incidental --- strong continuity fails on the larger $C_b[0,\infty)$ --- but
nothing below turns on it, and we take $C_0$ throughout.

Given a stationary diffusion $X_t$ driven by $\mu$ and $\Sigma$, one sets
$P_t(f)(x) = \int_{\R^n} f(y)\,P_{0,x}(t,dy)$. Under mild regularity on the transition
probabilities --- essentially that mass neither escapes to infinity in finite time nor moves a
macroscopic distance instantaneously, and that $P_tf$ is continuous --- the family $\{P_t\}$ is a
contraction semigroup on $C_0(\R^n)$, the continuous functions vanishing at infinity, and its
generator is the second-order elliptic operator
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{A}(f)(x) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^n \Sigma_{ij}(x)\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i\partial x_j}
+ \sum_{i=1}^n \mu_i(x)\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} .
\end{equation}
For Brownian motion, where $\Sigma = \mathbb{I}_{n\times n}$ and $\mu = 0$, this is
$\mathcal{A}f = \tfrac12\Delta f$.

We shall not develop the semigroup picture further, having obtained from it what we needed: the identification of the generator, and the confirmation that the $\tfrac12$ appearing in Itô's rule is the same $\tfrac12$ appearing in $\tfrac12\Delta$. It is worth recording, however, what the semigroup property actually encodes, because it is the first of the three approaches to fail in Part \ref{part:part2}. The identity $P_{t+s} = P_t \circ P_s$ is a restatement of the Markov property: the evolution over $t+s$ can be decomposed into evolution over $s$ followed by evolution over $t$, because at the intermediate instant the present state is a sufficient statistic for the future. When the driving noise has memory this decomposition is unavailable, the family $(P_t)$ is no longer a semigroup, and the generator ceases to exist as a local operator. Only the pathwise, integral formulation does, which is a large part of why the theory of Part \ref{part:part2} is built the way it is.

\vspace{0.3cm}

\textbf{\underline{2. Partial Differential Equations Approach}}
\vspace{0.2cm}

Let $P_{s,x}(t,\cdot)$ be the transition probabilities of a diffusion $X_t$ driven by $\mu$
and $\Sigma$, and let $F_{s,x}$ be the corresponding distribution function. The argument is a Taylor
expansion inside the Chapman--Kolmogorov equation
\[
F_{s,x}(t,y) = \int_{\R^n} F_{u,z}(t,y)\,dF_{s,x}(u,z),
\]
and runs as follows. Fix $t$ and $y$, put $u = s+\varepsilon$, and expand the integrand to second
order in $z$ about $x$. The zeroth-order term gives $F_{s+\varepsilon,x}$; the first-order term
contributes $\nabla_x F_{s+\varepsilon,x}\cdot\mu(s,x)\,\varepsilon$, since by Definition
\ref{def:diffusion}(ii) the mean displacement over $[s,s+\varepsilon]$ is $\mu(s,x)\varepsilon +
o(\varepsilon)$; and the second-order term contributes
$\tfrac12\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(D^2_x F_{s+\varepsilon,x}\,\Sigma(s,x)\bigr)\varepsilon$ by Definition
\ref{def:diffusion}(iii). Rearranging, dividing by $\varepsilon$ and letting $\varepsilon \to 0$
yields
\begin{equation}\label{Kolmogorov_backward}
    -\frac{\partial F_{s,x}}{\partial s} = \sum_{i=1}^n \mu_i \frac{\partial F_{s,x}}{\partial x_i} + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i,j=1}^n \Sigma_{ij} \frac{\partial^2 F_{s,x}}{\partial x_i \partial x_j},
\end{equation}
together with the terminal condition 
\begin{equation}
    \lim_{s\uparrow t} F_{s,x}(t,y) = \mathds{1}_{\{x \le y\}}.
\end{equation}



Equation (\ref{Kolmogorov_backward}) is called the \textbf{Kolmogorov backward equation}, because the
variable $s$ is the starting time, effectively moving backwards from the terminal time $t.$ It has a
converse that we shall not need until the next chapter but may as well announce here: equations of
this type are solved by expectations along the diffusion, which is the content of the Feynman--Kac
formula (Theorem \ref{thm:feynmankac} of Chapter \ref{ch:finance}), and \eqref{Kolmogorov_backward}
is its special case with no discounting.

We shall stay in the one-dimensional case for simplicity now. Assume the transition probabilities of a Markov process $X_t$ possess density functions, that is,

\begin{equation}
    P_{s,x}(t,dy) = p_{s,x}(t,y)\, dy,
\end{equation}
so that the Chapman--Kolmogorov equation takes the form 
\begin{equation}
    p_{s,x}(t,y) = \int_ {\R}p_{u,z}(t,y)p_{s,x}(u,z)\, dz.
\end{equation}
By using this identity and some auxiliary test functions (integration by parts), it can be proven that

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial p_{s_0,x_0}(t,y)}{\partial t}  = -\frac{\partial (\mu(t,y) p_{s_0,x_0}(t,y))}{\partial y} +\frac 1 2 \frac{\partial^2 (\Sigma(t,y)p_{s_0,x_0}(t,y))}{\partial y^2} \label{eq:fokkerplanck}
\end{equation}
which, together with the initial condition
\begin{equation}
    \lim_{t\downarrow s_0}p_{s_0,x_0}(t,y) = \delta_{x_0}(y)
\end{equation}
is known as either the \textbf{Kolmogorov forward equation} or the \textbf{Fokker--Planck equation.} This equation is much more useful in a financial context because it allows calculations \textit{forward} in time, i.e., evolving from known current market variables.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:kolmogorovwellposed}
    Let $\Sigma$ and $\mu$ satisfy the linear growth and Lipschitz conditions in $x.$ Assume, additionally, that there exists $c>0$ such that $w\transp\Sigma(t,y)w \geq c\lVert w \rVert ^2 $
for all $w\in \R^n$ (uniform ellipticity). Then:

\begin{itemize}
    \item[a)] The Kolmogorov backward equation has a unique solution $F_{s,x}(t,y)$. Moreover, there exists a continuous Markov process $X_t$ whose transition probabilities are recovered from it, $P_{s,x}(t,dy) = d_y F_{s,x}(t,y)$, the differential being taken in the second spatial argument.
    \item[b)] Assume additionally that the derivatives of $\mu$ and $\Sigma$ up to second order satisfy the Lipschitz and linear growth conditions. Then the Kolmogorov forward equation has a unique solution.
\end{itemize}
\hfill $\square$
\end{theorem}

In terms of the infinitesimal generator, we can summarise the previous discussion in the following table:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|}
\hline
\textbf{Forward Equation} & \textbf{Backward Equation} \\
\hline
$\displaystyle \partial p/\partial t = \mathcal A^* p$ &
$\displaystyle -\partial u/\partial s = \mathcal A u$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
where $\mathcal A^*$ represents the adjoint of the generator $\mathcal A$; see \cite{karatzasshreve1991} for the functional-analytic details. The duality is worth reading off the table: the backward equation propagates a \emph{payoff} backwards from maturity and is the natural object for pricing a single contract, whereas the forward equation propagates a \emph{density} forwards from today and is the natural object for calibration, since it evolves from known current market data. Dupire's formula in the next chapter (Subsection \ref{subsec:localvol}) is precisely an application of the second.
Figure \ref{fig:forwardbackward} shows the two evolutions for the geometric Brownian motion of the
example below.

\textbf{\underline{3. Stochastic Differential Equations Approach}}
\vspace{0.2cm}

In this case, take $\mu$, $\Sigma$ and $\sigma$ such that $\sigma \sigma\transp = \Sigma$. Notice that $\sigma U$ is also a valid solution for any matrix $U$ such that $UU\transp = \mathbb{I}_{n\times n}$ (orthogonal matrix). Therefore, the choice of $\sigma$ is unique up to multiplication by an orthogonal matrix. Since $U W(t)$ is also a Brownian motion, the transition probabilities of $X_t$ do not depend on the particular choice of $\sigma$ within its class. We call this class of matrices the \textit{square root} of $\Sigma.$

Now, suppose $\Sigma$ and $\mu$ satisfy the linear growth and Lipschitz conditions. It is clear that any square root of $\Sigma$ satisfies the growth condition. Moreover, if $\Sigma$ is uniformly elliptic (bounded away from zero), $\sigma$ also satisfies the Lipschitz conditions. In the 1D case, this can be seen via the inequality:

\begin{equation}
    |\sigma(t,x)-\sigma(t,y)| = \frac{|\Sigma(t,x)-\Sigma(t,y)|}{|\sigma(t,x)|+|\sigma(t,y)|}\leq \frac{|\Sigma(t,x)-\Sigma(t,y)|}{2\sqrt{c}}.
\end{equation}

Having this, we can directly state the main theorem corresponding to the SDE approach.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:sdeapproach}
    The unique solution to 
    \begin{equation}
        dX_t = \mu(t,X_t) \, dt + \sigma(t,X_t)\, dW_t, \quad X_{t_0} \ \mathcal{F}_{t_0}\text{-measurable}
    \end{equation}
    is a diffusion process, whose transition distribution function $F_{s,x}(t,y)$ satisfies the Kolmogorov backward equation. If the additional conditions on part $b)$ of the previous Theorem are satisfied, then $X_t$ has density functions $p_{s,x}(t,y)$, satisfying the corresponding Kolmogorov forward equation. \hfill $\square$
\end{theorem}

\begin{example}
We illustrate the previous discussion with the example that will occupy the whole of Chapter \ref{ch:finance}.
Take $n=1$ and consider geometric Brownian motion, $dX_t = \rf X_t\,dt + \sigma X_t\,dW_t$ on
$(0,\infty)$, so that $\mu(x) = \rf x$ and $\Sigma(x) = \sigma^2x^2$. By the theorem above its
generator is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{A}f(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 x^2 f''(x) + \rf\,x f'(x),
\end{equation}
and the backward equation $-\partial u/\partial s = \mathcal{A}u$ is, after adding the discounting effect, exactly the Black--Scholes equation \eqref{eq:BS_PDE} of
Section \ref{sec:blackscholes}. Its adjoint,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{A}^*p(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\bigl(\sigma^2x^2p(x)\bigr)
- \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\bigl(\rf x\,p(x)\bigr),
\end{equation}
governs the forward equation \eqref{eq:fokkerplanck}, and it is the object Dupire inverts in
Subsection \ref{subsec:localvol} to read a local volatility function off market data. The two
equations of the table are thus not an abstraction: they are the pricing equation and the calibration
equation of the entire Chapter \ref{ch:finance}.
\end{example}
\section*{Notes and references}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Notes and references}

\noindent
The theoretical framework of this chapter draws primarily from two authoritative sources. The general probability-theoretic material follows the classic exposition of P. Billingsley \cite{billingsley1995}, while the construction of the stochastic integral is based on the comprehensive treatment by H. Kuo \cite{kuo2006}. Throughout, the standard reference for the finer points --- the Daniell--Kolmogorov and Kolmogorov continuity theorems, the strong Markov property and the reflection principle, and the construction of the essential supremum used in Appendix \ref{app:esssup} --- is Karatzas and Shreve \cite{karatzasshreve1991}, which we cite at each of those points and recommend as the natural companion to this chapter and the next. Additionally, references for the underlying functional analysis, implicit in the PDE treatment of diffusions, can be found in the seminal work of H. Brezis \cite{brezis2011}.

Certain advanced topics, such as the general formulation of stochastic integration with respect to semimartingales or deeper pathwise properties of Brownian motion, have been omitted as they lie beyond the scope of this work. However, the selected contents provide a rigorous and sufficient foundation for the financial theory of Chapter \ref{ch:finance} and for the developments of Part \ref{part:part2}.
